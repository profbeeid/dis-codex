# Disney 4.0 — Crisis Architecture, Resource System, and Character Dossiers

**Disney Executive Crisis Committee · Civitas MUN · 5–6 September 2026**  
**Working design:** six delegates, three dais-held standing roles, seven committee sessions of 105–120 minutes  
**Purpose:** produce an unforgettable business game whose residue is three to five durable business truths

> Organizer document. Public record, institutional inference, and simulation canon must remain visibly separate. No invented private motive in this file is a claim about a living person.

# Part I — Deliberation and verdict

## 1. What the project has become

Disney 1.0 found the substance: Disney is not merely a media conglomerate but a machine that converts stories into attachment, attachment into participation, and participation into money across films, parks, products, streaming, and licensing. Its crisis is not that one quarter is weak. It is that the machine may still monetize inherited love while losing the ability to create fresh love.

Disney 2.0 built an information architecture: a study guide for the minimum shared world, a wiki for optional depth, private dossiers for asymmetry, and a dais engine for consequences. Disney 3.0 discovered the political physics: outsiders hold taps on resources; insiders fight over authority, budgets, succession, and blame; each external pressure becomes an internal weapon. Disney 4.0 then imposed the honest constraint: six students with little business instinct cannot run a miniature Fortune 500 bureaucracy merely because the organizers understand one.

The correct synthesis is not to make the world shallow. It is to keep the backstage model rich while making the player-facing interface small. Delegates should face three dials, a handful of concrete office resources, one sharp contradiction, and events that answer their actions. The dais may know the legal, financial, operational, and political machinery underneath. The delegates should discover it by collision.

The project should therefore stop thinking of itself as a Disney seminar interrupted by a game. It is a game that leaves business understanding behind as residue.

## 2. Evaluation of the study guide against 4.0

### What is excellent

The study guide contains a strong intellectual spine. The Snow White wager, the ABC–Disneyland bargain, the Renaissance, Save Disney, and the Disney+/Chapek/Iger succession sequence are not trivia. They teach recurring institutional moves: risk under uncertainty, turning a threat into a complement, creative deposits versus franchise withdrawals, governance failure, and the difficulty of replacing a founder-like leader. The guide also argues against its own monoculture thesis before rebuilding a narrower and stronger claim. That skepticism is rare and worth preserving.

Its best sentence-level insight is the difference between a bright scoreboard and a draining fuel tank. That idea can carry the whole simulation. Its best business model is the flywheel. Its best strategic question is not “How does Disney return to the past?” but “What must Disney stop being to remain alive?”

### What now fails

As a player-facing study guide, it is overqualified for the job. It asks students to absorb history, cultural theory, media economics, political conflict, several contemporary fronts, and a large bibliography before they have a usable action model. Much of the writing repeats the same thesis in increasingly elegant language. That makes it a good essay and a poor control surface.

The 4.0 invariants say that complexity must move from what delegates read to what they bump into. On that test, the guide fails. It explains the lesson before the game has a chance to teach it. It also gives sophisticated delegates a correct ideology to perform—long-term creative renewal—without forcing them to pay the cost of that ideology. A student can sound wise by defending originality while someone else pays the cash, delay, labor, and failure risk.

There are also production defects that matter because they erode trust: malformed phrases, pronoun substitutions such as “we” where “you/us” is intended, embedded citation artifacts, inconsistent sourcing density, and several precise contemporary numbers that need a final fact-check immediately before publication.

### Verdict

Do not delete it. Reclassify it.

- The full manuscript becomes the **Organizer Bible and optional long read**.
- The public study guide becomes **six to eight pages**, centered on the flywheel, the three dials, five historical decisions, the present leadership situation, and how to act.
- Each historical episode ends with one portable question, not an essay conclusion.
- The guide must not disclose the “correct” strategic answer. It should install vocabulary and contradictions, then stop.

The five history episodes are stronger as five one-page decision cases than as a continuous literary argument. Preserve the depth in linked optional notes.

## 3. Evaluation of the wiki against 4.0

### What is excellent

The wiki has the right delivery philosophy: static pages, sidebar navigation, plain-language explanations, a worked directive, office boundaries, and a clear firewall between public facts and private dossiers. Its best pages teach mechanisms rather than labels: what a distributor withholds, what a regulator can actually do, why licensing differs from ownership, how a film becomes a greenlit project, and why approval does not equal implementation.

The public office pages also correctly state boundaries. “Finance can price a choice but cannot decide what Disney should mean,” and “Legal advice defines options rather than wisdom,” are exactly the kind of lines that prevent offices from becoming magic vetoes.

### What now fails

The wiki still describes the old game. It presents seven dials after the design moved to three; it contains many offices that will not be live seats; it distributes attention evenly across areas the game will not use evenly; and its “basic skills” page resembles an executive curriculum rather than a just-in-time help desk. The page count rewards research but also gives anxious students thirty places to hide from acting.

The deeper problem is not length. It is the absence of routing. A reference desk is useful only when a player knows which drawer to open under pressure. The current wiki tells students what exists; it does not sufficiently tell them, “You are blocked because you lack implementation—open this page,” or, “You are trading reach for control—open this page.”

### Verdict

Keep the site and rewrite its information hierarchy around decisions:

1. **Play:** two action types, directive grammar, promises, firing, and reassignment.
2. **World:** Love, Money, Control; Patience and Peace as explanations of Control.
3. **Company:** the flywheel, one-page segment map, and decision-rights map.
4. **Your office:** only the six delegate offices and three standing external forces on the primary sidebar.
5. **When stuck:** five pages—money, implementation, law/permission, audience, negotiation.
6. **Archive:** all other business, history, and external-actor pages remain searchable or linked but leave the primary path.

The wiki should answer a live question in under ninety seconds. If a page cannot do that, it belongs in the archive.

## 4. The most important correction to the 4.0 foundation

The phrase “low capability, no business instinct” should be treated as a design constraint, not a judgment about what SMA students can learn. They do not need the game simplified into childishness. They need stairs and railings: visible causal chains, concrete resources, repeated decision grammar, and immediate consequences.

The realistic learning target is not merely two slogans. Across seven long sessions, three to five genuine concepts are attainable if three conditions hold:

1. the same concepts recur in different costumes;
2. the consequences remain causally legible; and
3. the room names the lesson after experiencing it.

Without the third condition, simulations often teach false folklore: that confidence beats evidence, betrayal is always optimal, or every failure was caused by a bad person. Experience does not automatically become understanding. Debrief is part of the engine.

# Part II — The 4.0 operating constitution

## 5. Love, Money, Control

### The three dials

| Dial | Plain meaning | What it is not | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Love** | People voluntarily choose, trust, remember, recommend, create with, and return to Disney | awareness, social-media noise, or one hit | repeat viewing, named-character demand, fan creation, retention, talent willingness, price tolerance |
| **Money** | Disney has the cash flow, financing capacity, and managerial bandwidth to keep acting | accounting profit or stock price alone | cash generation, committed spending, debt capacity, rights costs, operating margin, available teams |
| **Control** | Disney can make and preserve consequential choices without another actor dictating the terms | ownership on paper alone | IP rights, customer data, distribution access, regulatory permission, board authority, technical capability, credible alternatives |

### Patience and Peace are faces of Control

Do not track Patience and Peace as separate numbers.

- **Patience** answers: *Does Disney retain enough runway and credibility to wait for an uncertain bet to mature?* It falls when cash burns, investors lose trust, milestones are absent, or leadership becomes vulnerable.
- **Peace** answers: *Can Disney act without debilitating interference?* It falls when regulators, platforms, labor, internal coups, litigation, or partners can freeze execution.

When Control changes, the dais marks the cause as **PATIENCE**, **PEACE**, or both. This creates five felt pressures from three tracked values.

### The default trilemma

Most ordinary strategies preserve two dials while invoicing the third:

| Pair protected | Typical strategy | Hidden invoice |
|---|---|---|
| **Love + Money** | use a partner platform, license broadly, or exploit proven franchises | Disney loses data, rights, bargaining power, or the customer relationship: Control falls |
| **Love + Control** | fund original owned IP, protect talent, build owned capability | failure, time, and duplicated infrastructure consume Money |
| **Money + Control** | raise prices, cut experimentation, squeeze partners, defend the walled garden | the product becomes less loved, accessible, or culturally alive |

This is a default pressure, not a metaphysical law. A brilliant cross-office design may temporarily improve all three by removing a bottleneck—for example, a staged partnership with data rights, performance gates, and a buyout option. That exception is the game’s ceiling. If every move must lose something, delegates learn fatalism. If synthesis is possible but difficult, they learn strategy.

### Dial rules

1. Start within the middle band; suggested opening: **Love 48, Money 62, Control 56**.
2. Move a dial only when observable evidence changes. A persuasive speech does not move Love; a launched product, retained star, credible test, or public response might.
3. Ordinary outcomes move one dial by 2–6 points. A threshold event moves 7–12.
4. Every major decision gets an **immediate receipt** and a **delayed invoice**.
5. Publicly show values and the latest evidence. Keep formulas private.
6. Never let all three dials explain every event. Use the narrowest causal path.

## 6. Ten portable business principles

| Code | Principle | What delegates should be able to say afterward |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Value precedes monetization | “You cannot keep extracting from a customer relationship you are no longer renewing.” |
| **P2** | Cash and capacity are constraints | “A profitable idea can still fail because cash, people, or attention arrive at the wrong time.” |
| **P3** | Every choice has an opportunity cost | “Funding this means delaying or killing something else.” |
| **P4** | Metrics change behavior | “People optimize what is rewarded, sometimes by damaging what the metric was meant to represent.” |
| **P5** | Reach and control trade against each other | “A partner can make us faster and larger while making us dependent.” |
| **P6** | Fixed costs create operating leverage | “When a high-fixed-cost system loses volume, a small demand decline can become a large profit collapse.” |
| **P7** | Reversibility and sequencing create option value | “A staged decision can buy information before we make the irreversible commitment.” |
| **P8** | Authority is not implementation | “Approval is only the start; execution needs owners, skills, cooperation, and time.” |
| **P9** | Bargaining power comes from credible alternatives | “We negotiate well only when the other side believes we can wait, walk, or replace them.” |
| **P10** | Consequences compound through feedback and reputation | “A quick fix changes what customers, employees, partners, and investors expect from the next move.” |

The goal is not for every student to master all ten. Each role should repeatedly encounter three to five. The room as a whole contains all ten.

## 7. Two player-facing action types

### Public directive

A directive must state:

> **We will [action]. [office/person] owns delivery by [time], using [resource], with [required partner]. Success is [evidence]. We accept the risk of [named downside].**

### Private move

A private note must state:

> **I want [objective]. I am using [office authority, asset, relationship, or information]. I need [cooperation]. I am willing to risk [exposure, trust, delay, or resource].**

“I contact the press,” “I investigate,” and “I lobby” are intentions, not moves. The carrier of action is the move.

## 8. Seven-session arc

The strongest seven-crisis sequence is:

| Session | Major | Primary concepts | Why here |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | **M3 — The Blackout** | P5, P9, P8 | concrete negotiation tutorial; everyone understands access being cut |
| 2 | **M1 — The Empty Vault** | P1, P2, P3 | forces a portfolio choice after delegates understand actions |
| 3 | **M4 — The Golden Goose Revolts** | P2, P4, P6 | introduces operations, pricing, queues, and capacity |
| 4 | **M5 — The Synthetic Star** | P4, P5, P8 | turns technology into talent, rights, workflow, and incentives |
| 5 | **M6 — Permission to Speak** | P7, P8, P10 | introduces law, procedure, precedent, and political pressure |
| 6 | **M2 — The Rented Campfire** | P1, P5, P7, P9 | asks how Disney reaches children without surrendering its future |
| 7 | **M7 — Sell the Castle** | all, especially P3 and P10 | activist campaign and board vote force the room to price its accumulated choices |

M8, M9, and M10 are swaps or reserve sessions. M10 is also an emergency insert if the room becomes too abstract.

### Session rhythm: 115-minute target

| Minute | Beat |
|---:|---|
| 0–10 | cold open: public evidence and immediate deadline |
| 10–25 | private information and first bargaining |
| 25–45 | first directives and commitments |
| 45 | mini-crisis: the first receipt |
| 45–70 | implementation, coalition, counter-moves |
| 70 | midpoint reframe: an assumption becomes false |
| 70–95 | revised plans and sharper commitments |
| 95–105 | delayed invoice, leak, board reaction, or external response |
| 105–115 | “board minutes”: assumption → decision → outcome → principle |

For a 105-minute session, compress bargaining and implementation. For 120 minutes, expand the second response cycle. Do not fill extra time with more speeches.

# Part III — Ten overarching major crises

## M1 — The Empty Vault

Disney’s next three-year slate can fully support only two of three portfolios: proven franchises, original owned worlds, or creator-led/interactive experiments. A leaked audience study says nostalgia still converts among parents but under-twelve attachment to new Disney characters is weakening. A major original project has failed its first test but contains one segment audiences love.

**Business core:** exploration versus exploitation, portfolio construction, sunk cost, opportunity cost, staged commitment.  
**Trilemma:** franchise extraction protects Money and Control but spends Love; an owned original protects Love and Control but spends Money; a creator partnership may protect Love and Money but sacrifices Control.  
**Terminal choice:** one project is killed, one is staged, one is scaled.

Possible mini-crises: budget overrun; star exits; test audience segmentation reverses the headline; parks demands a character by a fixed construction deadline; merchandise buyers refuse the original; a sequel opens strongly then collapses in week two; a rival acquires the abandoned creator; the “failed” project becomes a meme.

## M2 — The Rented Campfire

A dominant game/social platform offers Disney immediate access to tens of millions of children. The platform will finance development and guarantee promotion, but demands behavioral data custody, broad remix rights, algorithmic control, and a long exclusivity window. Disney’s owned product cannot match the reach for at least three years.

**Business core:** build–buy–partner, platform dependence, data ownership, ecosystem strategy, real options.  
**Trilemma:** accept for Love and Money but lose Control; build for Love and Control but burn Money; refuse and protect Money/Control while Love migrates elsewhere.  
**Terminal choice:** accept, reject, or construct a staged hybrid with credible enforcement.

Possible mini-crises: child-safety allegation; algorithm demotes Disney content; a rival brand signs exclusivity; technical team misses a milestone; a fan-made world becomes more popular than official content; regulator questions child data; the partner offers a buyout clause; creators revolt against remix limits.

## M3 — The Blackout

A major distributor removes ESPN, ABC, and Disney entertainment channels during a high-value sports and advertising window. The public blames both sides. The distributor wants lower fees, in-product ESPN access, and custody of customer data. Disney’s direct product exists but cannot absorb all affected households.

**Business core:** bargaining power, outside options, channel conflict, customer ownership, switching costs.  
**Trilemma:** settle to protect Love and Money while losing Control; hold out for Money and Control while Love burns; migrate customers directly for Love and Control while paying acquisition and infrastructure costs.  
**Terminal choice:** a term sheet, a walk-away, or a customer-migration plan that can survive contact with operations.

Possible mini-crises: championship game approaches; advertisers demand rebates; app crashes under migration load; a competitor offers temporary carriage; league contract requires reach; leaked internal email contradicts Disney’s public line; churn data is worse than expected; distributor splits Disney’s coalition with a side offer.

## M4 — The Golden Goose Revolts

Parks revenue is strong, but guest complaints, employee fatigue, disability-access conflict, and price resistance converge just as Disney needs parks cash to fund the rest of the enterprise. A safety incident closes a high-capacity attraction and turns a manageable queue problem into a system problem.

**Business core:** pricing, capacity, bottlenecks, service operations, fixed costs, cross-subsidy.  
**Trilemma:** squeeze price for Money and Control while Love falls; cut price and invest for Love and Control while Money falls; outsource or sponsor capacity for Love and Money while Control falls.  
**Terminal choice:** a ninety-day operating plan with price, capacity, labor, and safety consequences.

Possible mini-crises: weather closure; employee absence spike; viral queue video; affluent guests threaten to defect if premium access is reduced; disability plaintiffs seek an injunction; hotel occupancy remains high while satisfaction falls; construction contractor demands a change order; a cheap operational fix damages the brand promise.

## M5 — The Synthetic Star

A generative-production system can cut animation and localization time dramatically. It was trained on disputed material, a performer’s digital likeness can be reused under ambiguous contract language, and creative labor fears permanent displacement. A competitor announces a fully synthetic hit at a fraction of Disney’s cost.

**Business core:** automation economics, complements versus substitution, intellectual-property rights, organizational incentives, capability building.  
**Trilemma:** deploy for Money and Control while risking Love; negotiate and build a trusted system for Love and Control while spending Money; license a third-party system for Love and Money while surrendering Control.  
**Terminal choice:** workflow, consent architecture, economics, audit, and kill switch—not a slogan about AI.

Possible mini-crises: model leak; performer injunction; animator sabotage allegation; synthetic scene tests better with one segment and worse with another; vendor raises prices; localization error causes offense; shareholders demand savings; star offers consent only for profit participation.

## M6 — Permission to Speak

The FCC’s early renewal process for ABC-owned stations intensifies while political figures publicly demand personnel and editorial changes. The legal probability of ultimate license loss may be low, but process, delay, chilling effects, and advertiser reaction are real. An internal document is legally defensible in context and disastrous as a screenshot.

**Business core:** regulation as process, precedent, option value, legal versus strategic risk, credible commitment.  
**Trilemma:** appease for Money and Peace while losing Love and institutional Control; fight for Love and Control while spending Money and Patience; restructure or sell for Money and Control while surrendering part of Disney’s public role.  
**Terminal choice:** litigate, negotiate, restructure, or combine them in a sequenced plan.

Possible mini-crises: regulator requests new documents; one commissioner dissents; advertiser pauses spending; station manager warns of employee exits; a host escalates publicly; board counsel raises fiduciary concerns; settlement intermediary lacks clear authority; a court grants only partial procedural relief.

## M7 — Sell the Castle

An activist launches a campaign to separate ESPN/linear assets, freeze selected long-term bets, return cash, and install board representation. The thesis is not obviously wrong. Disney’s accumulated session record gives both management and activist real evidence. The campaign triggers succession maneuvering and makes every broken promise relevant.

**Business core:** corporate governance, principal–agent conflict, portfolio logic, capital allocation, credibility.  
**Trilemma:** breakup may protect Money and focus Control while damaging Love/synergy; defend the empire for Love and Control while risking Money; negotiate for Love and Money while conceding governance Control.  
**Terminal choice:** the board votes on portfolio shape, capital plan, governance conditions, and leadership.

Possible mini-crises: proxy adviser recommendation; large shareholder defects; a division head privately supports separation; bidder appears; debt market reprices; employee petition invokes Disney identity; board minutes leak; activist accepts reform then adds a new demand.

## M8 — The Bargain That Eats the Buyer

A distressed interactive-entertainment or creator-network target offers reach, talent, and technology Disney lacks. The headline price is attractive. The true liabilities are culture, moderation, retention, integration, and an earn-out that rewards the target for behavior Disney may not want.

**Business core:** M&A, synergy, adverse selection, integration, incentives, post-merger accountability.  
**Trilemma:** acquire for Love and Control at a Money cost; partner for Love and Money with less Control; walk away to protect Money and Control while losing the capability race.  
**Terminal choice:** buy, partner, or walk—supported by an integration or alternative-build plan.

Possible mini-crises: founder threatens to leave; hidden moderation backlog; regulator raises antitrust concern; engineers demand retention grants; target’s users reject Disney ownership; rival bidder emerges; synergy estimate depends on double counting; culture audit reveals a solvable but expensive defect.

## M9 — Whose Magic?

An international park or content joint-venture partner demands local creative authority, pricing discretion, data access, and political accommodation. Disney’s global standards protect consistency but can make the product feel imported and unresponsive. Refusal risks market access; concession risks fragmentation and precedent.

**Business core:** joint ventures, localization, control rights, sovereign risk, standardization versus adaptation.  
**Trilemma:** localize for Love and Money while losing Control; insist on standards for Love-as-consistency and Control while risking Money; retreat to preserve Money/Control while abandoning future Love.  
**Terminal choice:** a governance matrix defining reserved rights, local rights, data, brand standards, escalation, and exit.

Possible mini-crises: partner appoints a political executive; local fan backlash; currency shock; censorship request; safety standard conflict; local creator offers a breakthrough idea; supply-chain disruption; another partner demands equal concessions.

## M10 — One Bad Night

A live-event, cyber, or safety failure hits multiple Disney systems at once. Facts are incomplete. Operations need speed, Legal needs evidence, Communications needs a truthful holding line, Finance sees mounting cost, and opportunists exploit confusion. The crisis exposes dependencies created by earlier decisions.

**Business core:** incident command, information asymmetry, resilience, accountability, crisis communication, recovery.  
**Trilemma:** shut systems to protect Love and Control while spending Money; continue operations for Money and Control while risking Love; outsource recovery for Love and Money while surrendering Control.  
**Terminal choice:** protect people, stabilize operations, preserve evidence, communicate, and redesign the failed system.

Possible mini-crises: casualty report changes; vendor denies responsibility; employee leaks a warning that was ignored; fake footage spreads; regulator orders preservation; insurer reserves rights; second system fails; a transparent admission improves trust while increasing legal exposure.

## 9. Mini-crisis design laws

The room does not need ten random emergencies per session. It needs five live consequences and two or three reserves. A useful mix is **70% consequences of delegate action, 20% pressure already visible in the world, 10% genuine surprise**.

Every mini-crisis should do at least one of six jobs:

1. **Reveal:** expose a hidden condition or better data.
2. **Constrain:** remove time, capacity, permission, or a partner.
3. **Reprice:** change the cost or value of an option.
4. **Delay:** prove that approval did not create implementation.
5. **Transfer:** move leverage, blame, or coalition power.
6. **Invoice:** deliver the delayed consequence of an earlier quick win.

Use these rules:

- Corner one or two seats and create an opening for another.
- Move one dial strongly or two weakly; never move all three by reflex.
- Preserve at least two viable responses. A quiz with one correct answer is not a crisis.
- Change one assumption at the midpoint, not the entire universe.
- Deliver information asymmetrically: public headline, private fragment, operational fact.
- Make delays and partial success common. Rejection is less educational than “accepted, but the missing dependency now matters.”
- Keep a visible causal sentence: **Because the room did X, actor Y can now do Z.**
- Do not fire every prepared card. Unused crises are evidence of restraint, not waste.

# Part IV — Resource tracking

## 10. The minimum viable resource system

The old ten-reservoir framework is useful for dossier design but too abstract for live play. Do not turn authority, trust, information, relationships, and disruption into ten currencies. Students will optimize tokens rather than exercise judgment.

Track three layers instead.

### Layer A — Public world state

The room display shows only:

- Love, Money, Control;
- the reason Control moved: Patience, Peace, or both;
- active deadlines;
- public commitments;
- the latest observable evidence.

### Layer B — Office resources

Each live role receives no more than five concrete resources:

1. **Standing power:** repeatable within jurisdiction.
2. **Implementation base:** the staff, platform, budget, contract, or system that makes action real.
3. **Information asset:** a report, model, relationship, or document others do not automatically possess.
4. **Scarce intervention:** powerful but exhausting, trust-limited, or once per session.
5. **Irreversible move:** firing, public break, litigation, shutdown, transaction, or disclosure.

Each resource card states:

> **Can do · Cannot do · Required partner · Time · Exposure · Refresh condition**

The resource has one of four states: **Ready, Committed, Exhausted, Compromised**. This is enough accounting.

### Layer C — Personal resources

Do not assign charisma or trust points. Track concrete relationships and receipts:

- who will take the call;
- who will speak candidly;
- who owes or is owed a promise;
- who has proof of contradiction;
- whose cooperation still looks legitimate.

The dais keeps a one-page **promise and betrayal ledger**. Trust changes permissions, not scores. A person may still cooperate after betrayal, but will demand security, publicity, or payment.

## 11. Resource adjudication

For each action, test four gates:

| Gate | Question | If missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority** | Who may approve or instruct this? | action becomes a proposal, unauthorized act, or governance breach |
| **Capacity** | Which people, cash, system, or contract carries it? | delay, reduced scale, quality failure, or displacement of another project |
| **Cooperation** | Whose consent or implementation is required? | bargaining, veto, workaround, or partial execution |
| **Clock** | When can evidence reasonably appear? | immediate promise, later result; impatience grows in between |

This teaches P8 better than any lecture. A directive that clears only Authority is not “rejected.” It is approved and stranded.

## 12. What should be public, private, and dais-only

| Public | Office-private | Dais-only |
|---|---|---|
| dial values; deadlines; public commitments; official decisions; known resource commitments | office cards; private goal; relationships; one contradiction; documents; current resource states | formulas; NPC thresholds; delayed invoices; causal ledger; reserve crises; board voting tendencies |

Do not display personal inventories or hidden probability. Uncertainty should come from incomplete information and human response, not opaque dice.

# Part V — What was still missing

## 13. Primary systems

### A terminal state

Session 7 should end in a board vote on a **Disney 2032 Compact**, not merely “CEO survives” or “dials remain above zero.” The compact has four linked clauses:

1. portfolio shape—what Disney owns, separates, partners, or exits;
2. capital rule—how much is protected for renewal and what milestones release it;
3. control rule—which rights/data/distribution dependencies Disney accepts;
4. governance rule—who leads, what oversight applies, and what triggers reconsideration.

The dials determine what options remain credible; they do not mechanically declare the winner.

### Learning capture

End every session with three minutes of individual writing and five minutes of board minutes:

> We assumed __. We chose __. The world answered __. The principle was __.

At the end of the event, ask each delegate to explain one concept through two different crises. Transfer across contexts is the proof that learning occurred.

### Award design

Reward causal reasoning, coalition, executable directives, and recovery. Do not reward verbosity, constant visibility, or simple dial maximization. Suggested weights: 25% consequence-aware judgment, 25% execution, 20% coalition, 20% adaptation, 10% communication.

### Firing and reassignment

At six delegates, firing cannot remove a player. **The person falls; the player continues.** The reassigned role should inherit a live resource and a grievance created by the dismissal. Examples: fired executive becomes activist ally, whistleblower, rival hire, board investigator, or external adviser. Reassignment occurs within ten minutes and includes one clear lever.

### Dais division

- **Chair/Rizky:** final judgment, pacing, terminal arc.
- **Dais 2:** state and causality—resources, dials, commitments, delayed invoices.
- **Dais 3:** voice and information—news, NPCs, private notes, public display.

A dais member never adjudicates an action taken by their own standing character. The chair resolves that action. Standing roles pressure; they do not author solutions.

### Tutorial and casting

Run a twenty-minute pre-crisis about a school festival vendor blackout or canceled venue. Teach the two note forms, the four resource gates, and the receipt/invoice pattern before Disney vocabulary arrives. Cast Mara Voss deliberately; the activist seat is too high-variance for lottery.

## 14. Secondary systems

- One authoritative visible timeline and one commitments ledger.
- No substantive WhatsApp politics; all consequential private moves must reach the dais.
- Printed one-page office cards even if digital tools exist.
- A fallback for absence, freeze, or early firing.
- A fact/simulation label on every contemporary claim involving living people.
- A content boundary for safety incidents, harassment, and political material appropriate to school participants.
- A dry run that tests whether the room generates plot without a dais character driving it.
- A post-event debrief artifact delegates can keep: the ten principles with examples from their own room.

# Part VI — Roster architecture

## 15. Interpretation of “core nine”

The 4.0 foundation names nine filled seats—six delegates plus three dais-held roles—but does not lock an exact named roster. The following is the cleanest playable interpretation.

### Six delegate-held seats

| Character | Function | Main collision |
|---|---|---|
| Josh D’Amaro | CEO / integrator | must own decisions without becoming Iger’s proxy or a board prisoner |
| Dana Walden | President & CCO / creative rival | must renew Love while working under the person who won the CEO succession |
| Hugh Johnston | CFO / capacity truth | must preserve Money without starving the future |
| Horacio Gutierrez | Legal & Global Affairs / option architect | must protect Control without turning risk into paralysis |
| James Gorman | Board Chair / kingmaker | must police leadership without becoming management |
| Mara Voss | activist investor / capital patience | must prove whether management’s long-term story is strategy or insulation |

### Three dais-held standing forces

| Character | Function | Steamroll limit |
|---|---|---|
| Evelyn Ward | FCC chair / permission pressure | asks for records, process, remedies, and deadlines; never tells Disney how to solve them |
| Avery Cole | marquee talent / Love and production pressure | can bargain, walk, organize, or speak; never supplies the creative strategy |

This lineup preserves the 4.0 collisions while turning “Rival Company” into the more educational Jordan Lee. Competitors remain dais NPCs. Robert Iger is too powerful as a continuous dais avatar; activate him as an expiring kingmaker whose involvement always creates a cost.

## 19. The causality ledger

The dais keeps one row per consequential action:

| Time | Actor | Action | Resource used | Dependency | Immediate receipt | Delayed invoice | Dial evidence | Promise created |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

At each session break, choose no more than three unpaid invoices to mature next. This keeps causality legible and stops the room becoming a punishment blender.

## 20. Final success test

The committee succeeds if, by the final hour:

1. every delegate can name a decision they caused and the world’s answer;
2. every delegate has needed at least two other offices;
3. at least one quick win produced a delayed cost;
4. at least one initially failed plan was recovered through adaptation;
5. each delegate can explain three principles without Disney-specific vocabulary;
6. the final compact is coherent enough that reasonable people can disagree about it.

The once-in-a-lifetime impression will not come from the number of explosions. It will come from recognition: the moment a student realizes the room remembers a promise they made four hours earlier, a partner uses it against them, and the only escape is to build a better bargain. That is when the company becomes real.
