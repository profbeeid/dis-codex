## 3\. The lineage in three phases

### 3.1 Disney 1.0 — substance, and the first architecture

**The reframe that started everything.** Your opening instinct was that the crisis is the FCC fight over ABC licences. That got demoted to a symptom. `\[D]` The real crisis is Disney's cultural irrelevance in an age of algorithmic fragmentation. Thesis: **Disney is winning the quarter and losing the century.** Financials fine, box office reclaimed, buybacks flowing — and roughly six in ten kids under 12 prefer YouTube to Disney+, children imprint on MrBeast and Roblox instead of Disney characters. The company that manufactured the 20th century's shared mythology is asset-rich and relevance-poor in a century that no longer shares myths.

**The flywheel diagnosis.** Disney's model needs a child to bond with a Disney-owned character first. Films, parks, merch, streaming all monetise that bond downstream. If algorithms route a child's first love to creators Disney does not own, the flywheel loses its input. Nostalgia from ageing fans is a flywheel winding down.

**First architecture (mostly superseded, listed because 4.0 keeps borrowing from it):**

* Three power types instead of one-delegate-one-vote: Voters (board, C-suite), Actors (external levers, no vote), Advisors (information only, hireable and fireable). `\[X]` at 6 players, but the underlying idea — asymmetric action menus — survives.
* Decision-rights map: operational moves are fast; capital and strategic moves need CEO plus CFO plus a board threshold; **removing the CEO needs a board majority — the coup engine.** `\[D]`
* Two loops: front room is public theatre, back room is private notes where power actually moves. The gap between what a delegate says and does is the game. `\[D]`
* Firing engine with a twist: a fired delegate is never eliminated, they are reassigned into a live threat — rival's CEO, activist's ally, hostile witness, leaking pundit. Firing creates a more dangerous enemy, which is both the real F500 lesson and the thing that stops firing from being a dominant strategy. `\[D]`
* Reputation as anti-chaos currency: break a public promise, the room learns, coalitions get harder. Betrayal stays possible, never free. `\[P]`
* Floor and ceiling: the floor is a promise — every delegate gets at least one moment where they acted and the world visibly answered. The ceiling is where reward concentrates — greatness pays more than competence. Do not equalise outcomes. `\[D]`

**Your three anxieties, reframed.** You feared you could not simulate a complex corporate environment; that corporate settings are less dramatically alive than war rooms; and that you needed to master Disney's full mechanics to chair. The reframe: the engine is **plausible fast improvisation, not faithful simulation**; the delegates generate the drama by colliding, not the calendar; and dais skill is **directional plausibility delivered with speed**, not accuracy. `\[D]`

**The scope-discipline scar.** The April–May software engine died from scope creep — one hardcoded Disney committee drifted into a universal configurable engine with five config layers. Standing rule since: one committee, human-run, paper first. The software ambition is quarantined until a paper version has actually run. `\[D]`

**Density benchmark:** Darendra's SMUN Taiwan KMT guide and wiki. `\[X]` for this cohort — see 3.3.

### 3.2 Disney 2.0 — the constitution and the delivery ecosystem

A 14-section MECE Crisis Constitution plus 12 ranked undecideds. Three delegate touchpoints, each with a different job:

* **Study guide (PDF)** — the minimum to play. Public board only, must not spoil the hidden-information game.
* **Wiki (GitHub Pages, repo `profbeeid/civitasmun-disney-wiki`)** — rewards the delegate who wants to win. Unlimited depth.
* **Sealed private dossiers** — per delegate, the actual lever, private goal, and secret.

**The five primitives** — any crisis committee reduces to these; everything else is decoration. `\[D]`

1. Actors — who is in the room, what they want, what they control
2. World state — a small set of dials; actions move them
3. Actions — public directives and private crisis notes
4. Information channels — front room, back room, hallways
5. Adjudicator — the dais as engine, not referee

**The pedagogy bet installed in the wiki.** SMA delegates are likely experienced GA chairs with no business intuition who will dump the study guide into a free AI and feel prepared. Counter: a "Before you play" cognitive bridge installing four reasoning engines — the 12-year Elsa revenue flywheel, deposit-versus-withdrawal for creative decisions, two-clocks short and long horizon, and a household analogy for segment economics — plus observable dials with four plain-language columns each (what it feels like, evidence it is moving, when it is dangerous, what moves it). Three worked examples: regulatory response, original film versus sequel, park expansion versus gaming.

**Failure modes named:** dead seats, external actors becoming furniture, dais throughput collapse, novice fired early and lost, opening essay too long, continuity breaks, and politicking migrating to WhatsApp where your log captures nothing.

**Build order (still valid):** lock mechanics and terminal state, write resource sheets and private briefs, write the public guide, pre-write only the first 3–4 crisis breaks, train the dais until rulings converge, dry run.

### 3.3 Disney 3.0 — the two-engine model, then the cut

This is the phase where the underlying physics got named. Almost all of it survives the cut as *understanding* even where it dies as *mechanics*.

**Two engines, one weld.** `\[D]` The room is two different games running at once.

* **The reservoir game (outsiders).** A company is a bundle of reservoirs it depends on. There are about four worth naming: money, permission, talent, legitimacy. An outsider is simply someone holding a tap on one of them, and has exactly two moves: close the tap or poison it. An activist closes money by organising sellers. The regulator closes permission. A star walking closes talent. A boycott poisons legitimacy. That is the whole external world. You never have to teach a kid what an activist investor *is* — you teach them "this person can make a lot of owners leave at once, and when owners leave the price crashes, and when the price crashes the board gets scared." The mechanism is the lesson; the costume is optional.
* **The tournament game (insiders).** Internally the pie is fixed: one throne, a set of budgets, a running blame ledger. Insiders compete over succession, turf, and routing blame toward whoever is least defended.
* **The weld.** The CEO sits on it — accountable to outsiders' taps, hunted by insiders' tournament. **Every tap an outsider pulls becomes a knife an insider can use.** That interlock is the game.

**Office as resource base, not mandate.** `\[D]` The Indonesia-style move. A seat's power is not the duty it is supposed to perform; it is the resource it controls and can distribute selectively. Power equals what your seat controls, times how well you personally wield it through favours, debts and loyalty. This converts corporate power (needs scripts SMA kids lack) into patronage power (needs only social intuition they already have). The constraint: favours must be traded over **real Disney levers**, or the game stops being about Disney.

**Eight power species.** Formal authority, resource control, veto, implementation, information, agenda, legitimacy, coalition. A playable seat holds one strongly and depends on others for the rest. Total independence kills politics; total dependence makes spectators. `\[P]`

**The six-question seat test.** `\[D]` A position earns a delegate seat only if a student, minutes after reading, can answer: What can I do that others cannot? What can I produce? Whose cooperation do I need? Who wants something incompatible with me? Why do I still matter in six months? How can I gain, lose, or transform my power? Fail it and it becomes a dais NPC, an adviser, or a resource — not a seat.

**The MECE world map and the missing bucket.** Four buckets: firm, exchange counterparties, contestants, field. The decomposition surfaced something absent from both of our working lists — **customers and fans**. Without them the game structurally cannot dramatise its own thesis, because "losing the century" *is* fan-attention migrating to competitors. Fans are too diffuse to be a seat, so they became **the slow dial**: Fandom drifts toward competitors every round unless fed by genuine creative risk, and it caps the final score if it dies. The fast financial dials are the clock the room panics over; Fandom is the clock that quietly bleeds while everyone fights fires. The trap is that defending the short clock — cut costs, milk the IP, demote the risky original — actively drains the long one. A kid who maxes cash and watches Love collapse has *lived* the thesis rather than read it. `\[D]`

**Design law, from the Carey experiential games.** `\[D]` Give one or two constraints. State a clear goal. Let the unstated space become the players' creativity. Complexity should emerge from few mechanics, not be shoved in as content. Clear goal, discovered means. Fun first; business understanding is the residue that hard play leaves behind. You teach a principle by choosing a mechanic whose emergent behaviour *is* the principle, then letting it happen to them.

**True analogues** for what you are building: Crusader Kings, Diplomacy, tabletop RPG dungeon-master craft, megagames like Watch the Skies. **Not** Civilization, Factorio, or The Sims. The dais runs as a DM with a stable-card system for outside forces, not as a referee.

**Then the cut (1 Aug).** New constraint: 6 delegates, low capability, no business instinct; the dais takes 3 standing roles to fill the room to 9. This killed most of the 3.0 machinery — the three-layer economy, extraction accounting, the tracked obligation ledger, Clout as points, the 16-actor roster. And it forced one honest concession: with genuinely no-instinct kids, "learn F500" mostly comes off the table. The realistic target is fun plus one or two residue principles — **"you cannot win alone"** and **"a quick win can cost you later."** Aim there, and do not let anyone sell you that six kids absorb capital-markets dynamics in a day.

## 5\. The invariants

Survived every rewrite including the cut. Break these and you are building a different thing.

1. **The floor is a promise, the ceiling is where reward concentrates.** No delegate gets zero; the best delegate gets far more than the competent one.
2. **Consequence is the memory mechanism.** People remember acting and watching the world answer. Nothing else in the design produces memory.
3. **The dais is an engine, not a referee.** Speed and directional plausibility beat accuracy. A mechanical outcome delivered flatly is not consequence; delivered as a two-sentence news flash, it is.
4. **The gap between public speech and private action is the game.** Front room theatre, back room power.
5. **Every seat needs three things or it is a dead chair:** a private goal, a lever, and at least one other seat whose goal collides with theirs.
6. **Behaviour from circumstance, not script.** "You are disloyal" is dead. "The board is quietly scouting you, and the boss will notice if you court directors" is alive.
7. **One sharp contradiction per seat, not ten secrets.** A pile of objectives produces paralysis.
8. **Power must travel on paper, not only through voice** — or the loudest kid becomes the real CEO regardless of title.
9. **Authority is always conditional.** Formal power is worthless without the votes, cash, clearance, and cooperation to use it. Approval is not implementation.
10. **Most actions transfer pressure; they do not remove it.** There is no universally happy move, only a choice of whose tap you protect.
11. **Everyone protects Disney sincerely, from a different feared failure.** Not good versus bad. The money seat fears insolvency, the creative seat fears killing the pipeline, the board fears the wrong successor, the raider fears prestige laundering underperformance. Divergent fears, not villainy.
12. **Reward recovery over invulnerability.** The kid whose plan shatters and who repurposes the wreckage outscores the kid who never got hit.
13. **Scope discipline.** Paper first. No software. The pull toward building a system instead of running a committee is the exact failure signature.

## 6\. Current design state — the six-player edition

`\[P]` throughout. This is the proposal you were left with, not a ratified design.

**Two dials.** Money (survive now) and Love (survive the future — the old Fandom dial). Any kid understands "we are going broke" and "people stopped liking us." Two clocks, two needles.

**Six kid-held seats, picked for collision rather than realism.**

|Seat|Plain identity|Built-in collision|
|-|-|-|
|The Boss|CEO|Everyone can undermine you|
|The Rival|Deputy who wants the job|Boss, over the throne|
|The Money-keeper|CFO, controls cash|Creative, over budget|
|The Creative|Studio head, only lifter of Love|Money-keeper|
|The Lawyer|Can stop things or shield people|The swing everyone courts|
|The Raider|Activist outsider, wants a break-up|The whole board|

Three built-in fights among six kids: Boss vs Rival, Money vs Creative, Raider vs everyone. Nobody is a spectator.

**Three dais-held roles — reactive outside pressure only.** The Government (presses on rules), The Star (famous talent who can walk, presses on Love), The Rival Company (steals fans, drives Love). These respond to what the kids do, so playing them never requires the dais to out-scheme anyone.

**The hard rule for yourselves.** Your three roles set problems; they never solve the room. A sophisticated dais playing a role will steamroll six clueless kids without meaning to and turn them into passengers. One licensed exception: if a kid freezes, an in-character nudge that hands agency *back* ("hey Money-keeper, what would that cost us?"). If you catch yourself driving the plot through your own role, stop.

**What each kid holds.** One personal goal on a card, plus the shared "do not let the company die." Two things. That is the ceiling for this room.

## 9\. Open decisions, ranked by dependency

The first two unlock most of the rest.

1. **Scaffold or pressure.** How much do the three dais roles carry the kids? Lean scaffold: they get carried and learn the shape faster. Lean pressure: more honest play, some kids flounder. This decides the tone of the whole manual. `\[O]`
2. **Terminal state.** What does hour 16 resolve? A board vote on the break-up? The Boss surviving or falling? Love above or below a line? Without this, 16 hours has no shape. Still open since 1.0. `\[O]`
3. **Merge Rival and Creative, or keep them separate?** See 8.1. Merging frees a seat for something the room lacks. `\[O]`
4. **What fills the freed seat, if any.** Candidates: a second Love-lifter, a press seat, or nothing. `\[O]`
5. **Real names or archetypes.** Corporate seats as real people has been standard; government seats were to be fictionalised for optics with parents. With six low-capability kids, archetype names ("The Boss") may carry better than "Josh D'Amaro." `\[O]`
6. **How the Love dial is fed.** Right now only the Creative can lift it, which makes that seat either dominant or decorative. `\[O]`
7. **Death rules at 6 players.** Firing removed 1 of 21 before; now it removes 1 of 6. The reassignment engine matters more, not less. `\[O]`
8. **Note channel.** Digital and logged, or verbal and fast. At six players, verbal is probably enough and the logging problem disappears. `\[O]`
9. **Awards and what the conference needs.** Never examined. What does Civitas need this committee to *produce* — a best delegate, a story, happy parents? That constraint has been invisible in all three phases. `\[O]`

## 10\. Devil's advocate — seven ways 4.0 fails

Written against the current proposal, not against you.

**10.1 Patronage needs clients, and you no longer have any.** The Indonesia-style frame is the most beautiful idea in the whole build, and it may not survive six players. Patronage is a *network* phenomenon: a patron needs a client base, clients need to compete for the patron's favour, and betrayal needs somewhere to hide. With five potential counterparties who can all see each other across one table, you do not have a patronage network — you have a friendship group. Everyone sees every deal. The information asymmetry that makes favours valuable requires a crowd. **Honest position: at six players, expect the game to behave like Diplomacy, not like Crusader Kings.** Design for the small-table dynamic — tight, personal, high-visibility betrayal — rather than trying to force patronage and getting a thin version of both.

**10.2 Your scarcity assumptions inverted and you have not re-run the maths.** Every design instinct from 1.0 and 2.0 was built against *attention scarcity*: 21 delegates, dead seats, kids drowning, dais throughput collapse. At six players over 16 hours the opposite is true. Each kid has roughly two and a half hours of floor time available. The failure mode flips from "some kids never act" to **"we ran out of game by hour six and the last ten hours were repetition."** This has a counterintuitive consequence: **your crisis deck needs to be bigger than the 21-player version, not smaller.** Six kids will burn through content far faster than twenty-one, because there is no queue. The strip-everything instinct is right for *rules* and wrong for *beats*. Budget at least one hard board-repricing event per hour, plus reserves.

**10.3 The dais's cognitive load went up, not down.** The 1.0 dais split into four capacities — Pulse, Voice, Machine, and judgement — because no one person could hold them. Now each of three people also plays a character continuously. Playing a role while adjudicating is a known trap in tabletop craft: the moment you are invested in your character's outcome, your rulings drift, and the kids can smell it. Mitigation: rotate so that the person voicing an external force is never the person resolving the action it responds to; and hold the non-negotiable from 3.0 — one shared visible state, divide by function not by table, and **never contradict each other in the room**, because a world that answers two ways teaches players it is fake.

**10.4 The Raider is a casting problem masquerading as a design.** One kid against five is the highest-variance seat in the game. Cast a confident kid and they become the protagonist; cast a shy one and the seat is dead by hour three and you have lost your only player-driven antagonist. Either build a fallback (the dais's Rival Company inherits the wolf function) or accept that this seat must be cast, not assigned by lottery.

**10.5 Two dials with one lifter makes one of them fake.** If only the Creative can move Love, then either the Creative is the most powerful seat in the room or Love is scenery. Both are bad. Give Love at least two live paths — one creative, one relational (the Star, fan-facing gestures, a public stance) — so the dial is contested rather than owned.

**10.6 "Fun first, learning as residue" is correct and also convenient.** It is the right call for this cohort. It is also exactly what someone would say who has spent a month building an elaborate system and now has to justify throwing it away. Keep the principle, but write down before the event what residue you are actually aiming for and how you will know. If your answer at hour 16 is "they had fun" with no observable second thing, the principle was a permission slip.

**10.7 The abandonment pattern has a new disguise.** The April–May engine died from scope creep. You caught it, quarantined it, and built paper. But 3.0 produced a full manual, a 16-actor dossier set, a crisis deck, and a dais manual — and then one message reduced the room to six kids and most of it became unusable. **That is not scope creep; it is building far ahead of a confirmed constraint.** Same abandonment cost, different mechanism. The cheap fix for 4.0: confirm the delegate count, the cohort, and the terminal state in writing before you write another manual. Those three facts have moved once already, five weeks out.

## What you want

Two things.

**Keep the company alive.** Everyone shares this one.

**End on top.** This one is yours alone, it is different from everybody else's, and it is in your envelope. Some of you can get what you want while the company does well. Some of you cannot.

## The three needles

**Money.** Can we pay for things.

**Love.** Does anybody actually care about us.

**Control.** Do we own our own future, or are we renting it from somebody bigger.

You will always be able to see which way each needle is moving. You will never see a number. Nobody in the room gets a number.

## The one rule

**Every time you act, you name the two needles you are serving. The third moves against you.**

Say it out loud. Write it on the directive. There is no move in this committee that serves all three, and if you think you have found one, look again at what it costs.

## The one thing to know before you start

Three of the four insiders, working together, can remove whoever is in charge. The outsider cannot vote on it. He can only make it worth doing.

## The ending

At the end of the second day something will be decided that cannot be undone, and all of you will have voted on it.

## Everything else

Work it out by doing it. That is deliberate, not laziness. Nobody is going to tell you which needle matters most, because nobody in that room agrees.

## What counts as an action

Anything you want to happen has to answer five questions. If it does not, the chair's table will hand it back and you will have lost the time.

* **What** is being done, in one sentence
* **Who** does it — a named person or office, not "the company"
* **What it needs** — money, permission, somebody's cooperation
* **When** it is finished, or when the first sign of it appears
* **Who can see it** — public, or only the people named

Vague directives are not rejected because the chair is strict. They are rejected because a real one would be. "We should improve our relationship with families" is not an action. "Cut single-day gate prices by fifteen percent at two parks from November, funded from the marketing budget, announced publicly" is.

## Action cards

You get **three cards each session.** The person in charge gets four.

Spend a card to **start** something. Submitting a directive costs a card. So does making an offer that binds you, or using your office to force something through.

**Free, always:** talking, arguing, persuading, asking questions, passing notes, and backing somebody else's action.

Unspent cards **do not carry over.** At the end of a session they are gone.

When your cards run out you are not silent. You are simply no longer the one making things happen, and everybody at the table can see it, because the cards sit face up in front of you.

## Two rooms

**The floor.** You speak, propose, argue, and look reasonable. Everything here is public and everybody hears it.

**Paper.** You write to anyone you want — at this table or outside it. The chair's table reads directives. It does not read your notes and does not want to.

What you say and what you arrange do not have to match. That is not cheating. That is the game.

## What only you can do

Each seat can grant **one thing per session** that nobody else can grant. It is in your brief. It is a permission, not a quantity — you either use it on somebody or you do not.

Nobody keeps a ledger of who owes whom. You are expected to remember, and to disagree about it.

## Promises

Break a promise you made in public, and the chair's table takes **one card off your next session**, in front of everyone, and says why.

Betrayal is always available. It is never free.

## Losing your seat

Nobody is eliminated from this committee.

If you are removed, fired, or walk out, you come back — as something considerably less convenient for whoever did it to you. The seat changes. You do not leave the room.

This is one small piece of one session. Nothing here is a hint about what will happen in yours.

**10:14** — The chair's table reads out that a government office has demanded documents about how the television stations are run. It gives a deadline. It does not say what happens if the deadline is missed.

**10:16** — Four people talk at once on the floor. Nobody has done anything yet. Talking is free.

**10:19** — The **Money-keeper** writes a note to the **Builder**: *if we fight this it costs us for months, back me and I'll fund your thing next session.* Costs nothing. Nobody else sees it. The chair's table does not see it either.

**10:23** — The **Boss** submits a directive. He spends a card.

> Hand over the documents, in full, by the deadline. Legal team leads. Announce nothing publicly. Declaring \\\*\\\*Money and Love\\\*\\\*.

**10:25** — The chair's table asks one question: *who signs this, and what do we say if a journalist asks before the deadline?* The directive goes back. No card is returned. This is normal and it happens to everyone.

**10:29** — Resubmitted with a name and a line to use publicly. Accepted.

**10:31** — **Control moves down.** It was declared. Everybody watched him declare it.

**10:34** — The **Raider** stands up and says the company has just admitted it does not control its own broadcasting. He is not wrong. He spent nothing to say it, because talking is free.

**11:02** — Half an hour later, a consequence arrives that nobody connected to the 10:23 decision until it lands.

Everything in a session works like this. Somebody acts, it costs a card, the world answers immediately in a small way, and then answers again later in a way that is harder to see coming.

## Words about the game

**The floor** — public discussion. Everybody hears it.

**Note** — anything written on paper to a named person. Private. Free. Not read by the chair's table.

**Directive** — an official instruction submitted to the chair's table. Costs a card. Must answer the five questions.

**Declaring** — naming the two needles your action serves. Required. The third moves against you.

**Action card** — three per session, four for the person in charge. Spend to start something. Expire at the end of the session.

**Grant** — the one thing your seat can give somebody, once per session.

**The dais** — the chair's table. Runs the world, rules on directives, and plays everyone outside the room.

**Insider** — the five people who work for the company. **Outsider** — the one who does not.

**Removal** — three of the four insiders can remove whoever is in charge. It is a vote, it is public, and the outsider does not get one.

## Words people will say in the room

**Activist investor** — somebody who buys a piece of a company in order to force it to change, usually by breaking it up and selling the parts.

**Licence** — government permission for a television station to exist. It can be reviewed. That review is itself a weapon, whatever the outcome.

**Carriage** — the fee a cable or streaming service pays to carry our channels. When the two sides cannot agree, the channels go dark.

**Streaming** — selling directly to viewers through an app. More control, worse economics than the old system it replaced.

**Franchise** — a story world we already own and keep returning to. Reliable. Gets less valuable each time.

**IP** — intellectual property. The characters and stories we own. The core asset, and increasingly the crutch.

**Box office** — money taken by films in cinemas.

**Buyback** — spending our own cash to buy our own shares. Makes this year's numbers look better. Spends money that could have funded something new.

**The quarter** — three months. The unit the outside world grades us on, and the reason most bad decisions get made.

## The Platform

A technology company considerably larger than this one. It is not hostile. It has never been hostile. Roughly once a session it arrives with a specific, generous, carefully reasoned offer, and every offer is genuinely good on two of your three needles and quietly expensive on the third.

It never threatens and it never rushes you. If you refuse, it takes that gracefully and comes back next session with something better. If you refuse three times, it makes the same offer to somebody else and you read about it.

The most dangerous thing about the Platform is that it is nice.

## The Government

A regulator that mostly does not decide anything. It requests, it schedules, it extends deadlines, it asks for more documents, and it does all of this politely.

You will probably win in the end. It will take four years, during which everything continues exactly as it is, except that you are spending time and money and attention on it, and everybody watching has learned that you can be made to do that.

Being right and winning are two different situations. You will have to decide which one you are trying to be in.

## The Star

A creator whose name sells the biggest thing this company owns. Court them and Love rises. Ignore them and it falls. Lose them and you find out how much of the thing was ever yours.

They are not difficult for the sake of it. They are usually right about something the room got wrong, which is what makes them hard to dismiss and expensive to keep.

## The drift

**Love falls every session unless somebody has spent something on it.**

Nobody has to do anything for this to happen. There is no villain attached to it. It is happening while you argue about other things, and it will keep happening, and by the time it is obvious it has usually been true for a while.

## Everybody else

There is a world beyond those three. Journalists who are accurate and slightly ahead of you. Bankers who are polite about the deadline right up until the deadline. Staff who make the thing and are not angry, only tired. Directors, distributors, lawyers, and the man who ran this company for twenty years and has not entirely left the building.

They appear when they are relevant. You can also ask to speak to any of them — write a note to the chair's table saying who and why. Sometimes they are available. Sometimes they are not, and the reason is itself information.

## There is a final vote

On the second day, something will be decided that cannot be undone, and all of you will have voted on it. It will be public. Nobody will be able to abstain quietly.

You will not be told in advance what the question is. You will be able to see it coming for about a session before it arrives, if you are paying attention to the right things.

## Two questions decide how you finish

**Did the company come through this in a shape you can live with?**

**Did you get the thing in your envelope?**

Those are not the same question. For some of you they point in the same direction and your job is simply hard. For some of you they do not, and your job is something else entirely. Nobody will tell you which kind you are. You will work it out somewhere in the second session, and the way you find out will probably be uncomfortable.

## Losing your seat is not the end

If you are removed, fired, or you walk out, you come back. You come back somewhere less convenient for whoever did it to you, and you can still get what was in your envelope. Sometimes more easily than before.

Nobody is eliminated from this committee. People are only relocated.

## Nobody gets everything

There is no ending in which all six of you are satisfied. The needles do not allow it and neither do the envelopes. Somebody at that table wants something that can only happen if you do not get what you want, and by the second day you will know who.

## The thing actually worth knowing

Something you try will fail, probably in public, probably early.

That is not the interesting part. What you do in the forty minutes afterwards is the interesting part, and it is what the chair's table will be watching most closely.
