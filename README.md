# The Disney Machine

## The complete delegate rules

Ten of you are playing the ten most powerful people inside one enormous company. You sit at one table. Problems, deadlines and disasters arrive, and the company must answer them. Only the ten of you can assemble those answers.

You do not need to memorise this page. Read it once in order before the first committee session. During play, use the search box or the links below whenever you need an answer.

## The three sentences that run the game

### Nobody can finish anything alone

Every office controls something other offices need, and every office lacks several things controlled by somebody else. Your task is not merely to invent a good idea. Your task is to discover who holds the missing pieces and get them to put those pieces behind your proposal.

The CEO cannot make a film, commit money, define Marvel canon, place something on the app or certify a ride as safe. The CFO controls funding but does not control what the company makes. The lawyer can identify a lawful route but cannot supply the facts, money or machinery needed to execute it. Formal authority, practical capability and other people's belief in you are different kinds of power.

When somebody blocks you, begin with the assumption that their office is doing its job. If two delegates swapped chairs, they would often have the same argument from opposite sides. The conflict comes from the structure of the table, not necessarily from the person.

### Talking is free; paper is real

You may promise, bargain and agree verbally. None of that changes Disney. Only a **Directive** filed at the dais changes the company. Notes and conversations help you build a deal. A signed Promise Note can bind a future favour. The Directive turns the plan into corporate action.

Two people can have a perfect conversation, shake hands and still accomplish nothing if nobody writes the decision down, places the required Civ cards on it and walks it to the dais before the deadline.

### No is a normal answer

You may refuse a request without giving a reason. You may also say, “Yes, but what do I get?” A signature places your own office at risk while the lead receives the reward. Giving every signature away for free is not cooperation; it is donating your limited capacity to somebody else's project.

Your office brief prints the normal price of your yes. Read that line aloud. You do not need to sound aggressive. You only need to avoid giving away the thing that other people need from you.

## What Disney is

For this simulation, picture Disney as five businesses reaching into the same shelf of characters and worlds.

Films make movies for cinemas. Television and news operate ABC, local stations, news and television series. Streaming and product run Disney+, Hulu and the technology that delivers them. Experiences operates parks, cruises, hotels and merchandise. ESPN operates sport.

The five businesses share characters, dates, money, attention and public trust. A film may create the character used by a ride. Television promotes the film. The app gives it a second life. Merchandise sells it. ESPN may advertise it during a game. A change in one business therefore changes other people's plans.

Disney made about **$94 billion in revenue** and **$17.6 billion in profit** in the year represented by the simulation. Parks, cruises and products contributed about **$10.0 billion** of that profit. Films, television and streaming together contributed about **$4.7 billion**, while sport contributed about **$2.9 billion**. Disney+ and Hulu together held about **196 million subscriptions**.

The stories are created in one place while much of the money is made somewhere else. Experiences needs stories worth building. The story businesses need the money that Experiences helps produce. Neither side can simply walk away.

Dates also behave like scarce assets. Films are planned years ahead, attractions can take five to eight years, and sports-rights agreements can last a decade. Moving one date can break promotion, merchandise, app placement, talent and construction plans built on top of it.

Finally, Disney's emotional connection with audiences is an economic resource. People may experience an ordinary price increase as inconvenience, but a Disney decision can feel like betrayal. That is why **Love** is one of the company's three visible health dials.

## What is on the table

### Use the words precisely

In this game, **card** means one thing only: a Civ card. Scheduled work on the screen is a **Calendar Item**. Unscheduled work is a **Crisis Item**. Your public A4 page is your **Office Brief**. Your secret page is your **Private Document**. The chair's desk is the **dais**, and it is where cards come from and return. There is no bank and the Civ cards are not cash.

A **committee session**, often called a **comses**, is a block of real conference time. A **month** is simulated time inside Disney. A comses may contain one or two months, so rules that reset per comses do not reset at every Monthly Close.

### Civ cards

You begin with **ten Civ cards** in an open pile. Draw a large circle on one face of each. Circle up means the card works. Circle down means the card is damaged and does nothing. Cards represent your office's people, attention and credibility at the same time. There is **no limit** on how many cards you may hold.

You may do only three things with a Civ card. You may place one on a Directive as a signature. You may give one to somebody or receive one as part of a deal. You may spend cards on repair: **two cards repair one of your own damaged cards; one card repairs one damaged card belonging to somebody else**.

At every comses opening, each delegate repairs one damaged card for free. Successful Directives reward their lead. The CEO distributes five cards publicly at each comses opening. Delegates may also transfer cards through trades. There is no automatic card simply because a month passed.

Cards do nothing while untouched in your pile. At the same time, signing recklessly can destroy your office. Use cards; do not treat them as points to hoard.

### The Office Brief

Your Office Brief is public. Anyone may read anyone else's. It tells you what you decide alone, whose yes you need, who needs your yes, the normal price of your yes, facts only your office knows, your Override, what happens if your office breaks and the four questions.

Read your own brief before the first comses. During play, read the briefs of the people who hold what you lack and the people who need what you control. If you are nervous and cannot think of a contribution, read one of your “what only I know” facts aloud. It is already true, useful and exclusive to your office.

### The four questions

Every Office Brief contains the same four questions. You may ask them at any time. They are free, need no permission and need no paper.

> **1. My yes on this costs [the price printed on my brief].**  
> **2. What number is that based on?**  
> **3. Who outside this room has agreed to that?**  
> **4. What happens if it does not work?**

Question four usually points everybody back to the Calendar Item, where the default is already printed. Use these questions when somebody is confident, fast or difficult to understand. Reading a prepared question is a complete intervention; you do not need to invent a speech.

## Directives: how decisions become real

### What a Directive contains

A Directive is the only object that changes the company. The handwritten form identifies the Directive number, simulated month, filing time and lead. The action itself has only three lines, but those lines must contain:

1. **A number.**
2. **A person outside the room.**
3. **A date.**

The lead must also circle the dial the action costs: **Cash, Love or Control**. Every real decision costs something. A Directive with no circled dial is returned for revision.

Do not invent numbers and dates unnecessarily. Calendar Items already print them. Copy the relevant facts from the screen, identify the outside person involved, describe the action and collect the required signatures.

### How to file a Directive

First, identify the Calendar Item or problem you are answering. Read its **DECIDE**, **NEEDS**, **IF NOBODY FILES** and **DEADLINE** lines. Speak with every office whose consent is required and negotiate the price of their signature.

Second, write the Directive. Include the number, outside person, date and circled dial. Put one working card of your own on the paper as lead. Each required consenting office puts one working card on the same paper.

Third, physically walk the paper to the dais. Filing is public; the room can see who joined the action. **The time the Directive reaches the dais is what counts**, not the later time when the dais reads it.

The price is simple: **one card from the lead, plus one card from each office whose consent is required**. No other Directive-cost arithmetic exists.

### The four dais answers

The dais does not judge whether your idea is clever. It checks whether the decision has the necessary owners and required information.

**PRICED** means everything required is present. The Directive becomes live.

**WAITING ON [name]** means a required consent owner has not signed. Go to that person, negotiate and return with their card on the paper.

**REVISE** means the paper lacks a number, a date, an outside person or a circled dial. Fix the missing field and return. Revision itself costs nothing.

**IMPOSSIBLE** means there is genuinely no route. It should be rare.

A wild proposal is not rejected merely for being wild. If it requires something controlled by another office and that office has not signed, the ruling is WAITING ON that office.

### What happens after filing

A Directive may remain live before it resolves. If it succeeds, every signer receives the card they placed on the paper, and the **lead alone** receives one extra card from the dais.

If it fails, every card placed on the Directive goes to the dais. Every signer, including the lead, must also flip one additional card face down. A failed signature therefore costs two cards: the card on the paper and another card damaged in your own pile.

You may file early against a revealed future Calendar Item. The Directive is priced when filed but does not resolve until its month arrives. The Calendar Item's printed default still fires at its deadline if the Directive has not solved the problem by then.

### Signing and the price of your yes

Signing creates real downside and almost no private upside. On success you recover your stake, while the lead receives the bonus. On failure you lose your stake and damage another card. Delegates who did not sign still enjoy whatever benefit the successful action created for the company.

Therefore, say your price before deciding whether to sign. Ask for a card, date, character, placement, air time, public credit, written concession, future support or whatever your public Office Brief says your office normally values. If the lead will not meet the price, you may refuse.

You may sign at most **two Directives per committee session**. The limit does not reset at a Monthly Close. If one comses contains two simulated months, your two signatures must cover both.

### Filing is different from talking

A verbal agreement does not change Disney. A private note does not change Disney. Even a Promise Note binds a future favour rather than implementing corporate action. If the company must actually do something, somebody must write and file a Directive.

## The Override

Each Office Brief contains one office-specific Override sentence:

> “I will __________ now, and deal with __________ later.”

An Override forces a Directive through without the consent signatures of the offices you go around. Read the completed sentence aloud and file the Directive without those signatures.

The Override removes only one possible ruling: **WAITING ON**. It does not cure REVISE, so the Directive still needs a number, a date, an outside person and a circled dial. It does not cure IMPOSSIBLE. It may **never** be used on a Crisis Item.

Both blanks must come from something already visible in the room. The first comes from a live Calendar Item. The second identifies the visible person, date or obligation you are pushing aside. Your Office Brief also prints an example you may use word for word.

The cost is public and exact. For every consent office you skip, flip one of your own cards down. Each skipped office also flips one of its cards down. You do not choose victims: the victims are the consent owners you bypassed. You also lose your free repair at the next comses opening.

Ordinary Directive risk still applies. The lead places one card on the Directive. If it fails, that card is lost and the lead flips another card down. Because there are no consent signers, the lead carries the failure alone.

You may use an Override **once on Saturday and once on Sunday**. An unused Saturday Override does not carry into Sunday. You cannot use one when you lack enough working cards to pay its cost.

An Override solves the immediate consent problem by damaging the exact people whose cooperation you are likely to need later. Use it as a deliberate exchange, not as a free emergency exit.

## Promises, notes and hidden leverage

### Promise Notes

A Promise Note is **utang budi written down**. Write the exact future action and roughly when it will happen. Both people sign; the receiver keeps the slip. When the receiver presents it, the maker must perform the promise if it lies within the maker's authority.

“I will help you later” is too vague to bind anything. “I will support your next park Directive in November” is specific enough. A Promise Note makes your future tradable when you lack something useful today.

Each delegate receives **two Promise Notes for the entire weekend**.

### Private notes

You may pass small written notes directly to another delegate. The dais does not read them. Use them to offer, ask, warn or negotiate privately. A note remains talk: it binds nobody and changes nothing until converted into a Directive or Promise Note.

### Blackmail Items

Only three Blackmail Items exist, held secretly by three delegates. A holder may not display one publicly before using it. The holder may trade it for anything except Civ cards, use it according to the words printed on it, or keep it unused for the entire weekend.

## Calendar Items and Crisis Items

### Calendar Items

A Calendar Item is scheduled work on the screen. It contains a situation, a named owner, the decision required, the people whose consent is needed, the consequence if nobody files, a dial effect and a deadline.

At each comses opening, the dais reveals the Calendar Items for the **following** committee session. You therefore see current work and future work at the same time. Read ahead. You may negotiate, buy a date, write a Promise Note or file a future Directive before the item becomes current.

The default is not a discretionary punishment. If nobody solves the item by its deadline, the printed consequence happens. Deliberately accepting a default is allowed. Say that choice aloud and identify what it will do. Freezing until the same default happens is not the same as choosing it.

### When your name appears

Read all four functional lines: **DECIDE**, **NEEDS**, **IF NOBODY FILES** and **DEADLINE**. Then say, “I need [the people on the NEEDS line]. I am going to talk to them.” Stand up and visit those people. That is a complete and competent first move even before you have a plan.

If your name is not the owner, check the NEEDS lines anyway. Your consent may be essential to somebody else's item. If two people are arguing, check whether either plan needs your yes. You may already be part of the dispute without noticing.

### Crisis Items

A Crisis Item appears in bright yellow without advance warning and carries a short clock. It differs mechanically from a Calendar Item in two ways:

1. It requires **three consent signatures**, plus the lead's own card.
2. It may move **two dials down** instead of one.

Overrides cannot be used on Crisis Items. When one lands, find the NEEDS line and walk to those people. If you are not named there, use the four questions on whoever is responsible.

## Committee sessions, months and Closes

### Two clocks

Seven committee sessions occur across the weekend. Inside them, Disney experiences ten simulated months. Roughly one hour of committee time equals one month, and each simulated month ends in a Monthly Close.

| Comses | Conference time | Simulated months |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Saturday 10.30–13.00 | Months 1 and 2 |
| 2 | Saturday 14.00–16.00 | Months 3 and 4 |
| 3 | Saturday 16.30–18.00 | Month 5 |
| 4 | Sunday 08.30–10.00 | Month 6 |
| 5 | Sunday 10.15–12.00 | Months 7 and 8 |
| 6 | Sunday 13.00–14.30 | Month 9 |
| 7 | Sunday 14.45–15.45 | Month 10 |

### What resets when

At each **committee-session opening**, every delegate receives one free repair; every delegate's two-signature limit and challenge become available again; and the CEO receives five cards to distribute publicly.

The Override resets **once per day**, with no Saturday carry-over.

Each **month** ends in a Monthly Close. Defaults fire, dials move and live work reports.

Your two Promise Notes and any Blackmail Item last for the **whole weekend**.

### The comses opening

Before the first month in a comses, every delegate flips one damaged card up for free, except a delegate whose Override cost removed that repair. The CEO receives five cards from the dais and distributes them publicly. The dais reveals Calendar Items for the next comses. Then the current month begins.

### The month opening and briefing

One or two Calendar Items become live. Their owners speak first. The briefing should take about ninety seconds and answer six things already available on the item or Office Brief:

1. How large the problem is, using a number.
2. When it is due.
3. Who outside the room is involved.
4. What connects the problem to the bad outcome.
5. What the room must decide now.
6. The most important fact the owner does not know.

Admitting the missing fact yourself prevents somebody else from weaponising it.

### The challenge

After the briefing, one other delegate may identify one specific contradiction and cite its source: an Office Brief, the study guide, the screen or a Directive on the spike. The chair decides only whether the source actually supports the challenge.

If the challenge holds, the challenger receives one card and writes the requirement line instead. If it fails, the reverse applies. Each delegate may challenge once per comses. The opportunity goes to the first person who has not yet challenged, not simply the loudest or the first raised hand.

### Shopping and filing

Shopping normally takes ten to twenty minutes. Stand up, approach a specific person, explain what you want and ask their price. Pay, negotiate, offer something else or bring in the person who controls what they want. If you cannot become the buyer, become the broker of a three-way deal.

Write the Directive, gather the cards physically on the paper and walk it to the dais before the deadline. A Crisis Item may interrupt this work at any time.

### The Monthly Close

The Monthly Close is a management report, not a payout. The dais distributes nothing merely because a Close occurred.

The clock stops. Every unresolved Calendar Item fires its printed default, with the reason stated aloud. Everyone with live work reports what happened; anyone with nothing live says so. Crises, breaks and scheduled events resolve. The month's Directives are shown, and resolved Directives go onto the spike.

The CEO then gives the board a spoken report of under one minute: what Disney decided, what succeeded, what failed and what must be settled next month. If any dial stands at two or below, the board asks questions. Then the simulated month rolls forward.

When this was the final month of a comses, the comses ends. The next one begins with free repairs, renewed signature and challenge limits, the CEO's five-card distribution and the next future-item reveal.

## Damage, repair and breaking

A face-down Civ card remains in your pile but does nothing. Repairing your own damage costs two working cards. Repairing one card for somebody else costs one. The rules deliberately make mutual repair cheaper than isolated recovery.

If you must flip a card down and no working card remains, your office **breaks**. The consequence printed on your Office Brief is read aloud, one dial moves and your special power switches off until the next Monthly Close.

### Breaking

You remain in the game. You may speak, bargain, plan and receive cards. Other offices still need your permission, so a broken office becomes a wall rather than a spectator.

Your function returns at the next Monthly Close. At the next comses opening, you receive the ordinary free repair. If nobody has repaired you by then, the dais flips one card up regardless, so your office cannot remain permanently broken.

Only one office can be broken at a time. If another office would break while one is already broken, the second office flips one card and continues.

Damage spreads through dependencies. A badly damaged office becomes reluctant or unable to sign; plans requiring it stall; their leads seek shortcuts; the shortcuts damage more offices. Repairing a colleague is therefore not charity. It protects the machinery your own office expects to use later.

## Cash, Love and Control

The three dials show Disney's shared health.

**Cash starts at 7.** It represents what the company can afford.

**Love starts at 5.** It represents the warmth and trust of audiences, fans, families and talent.

**Control starts at 5.** It represents whether Disney is steering itself or being steered by the board, government, partners or press.

Dials rise when events go well. They fall when defaults land or when successful Directives impose their chosen cost. Every Directive must circle one dial because there is no costless decision.

When a dial reaches **two or below**, the board begins questioning the room and may restrict someone's authority. Nobody is removed from the simulation.

The dials force value choices. Protecting one may damage another. The problem is therefore not to maximise a single score but to decide which harm the company can carry.

## The ten outside offices

One chair plays all ten outside offices. Write an outside person's name on a Directive or say aloud that you need that office. Because every Directive requires an outside person, needing an outsider often means first needing the colleague who controls the route to them.

### The Board

The Board controls leadership and approves decisions large enough to change what Disney fundamentally is. Reach it through D'Amaro, or through Johnston carrying a number. It wants a company visibly run by somebody. It will not tell the room what to decide, but may later decide that the room chose wrongly.

### Imagineering

Imagineering turns a story into something safe and maintainable for twenty years. Reach it through Mazloum. It wants time and an early, clear definition of what the thing must be. It will not build faster merely because a date moved; it will build less.

### People

People determines whether a reorganisation leaves enough capability to work on Monday. Reach it through Johnston or D'Amaro. It wants consultation before a decision. It cannot restore people who have already left.

### Communications and Government Relations

This office controls Disney's official voice and its government relationships. Reach it through D'Amaro or Gutierrez. It wants to know before the public knows. It cannot make a bad decision good; it can only choose which truthful explanation appears first.

### Advertising and Distribution

This office operates the commercial machinery connecting films, sport and shows to audiences and buyers. Reach it through O'Connell or Pitaro. It needs certainty early enough to sell against. It cannot manufacture an audience that does not exist.

### The NFL

The NFL owns the games and approximately a tenth of ESPN. Reach it only through Pitaro. It wants improved terms, more of its programming on Disney channels and recognition as a partner. It will never sell the games outright; Disney rents the rights.

### Comcast and NBCUniversal

Comcast controls relevant Hulu consent, carriage and a competing streaming service. Reach it through Smith with Gutierrez, or through Johnston. It wants payment without strengthening a rival. It may trade with Disney, but it will not help Disney win.

### Talent and Guilds

Talent and Guilds control the people legally able to write, act, direct and promote Disney's products. Reach them through Walden. They want respect, credit, money and no surprises. They remember the last negotiation when entering the next one.

### Washington

Washington controls regulatory permission, investigations and conditions. Reach it through Gutierrez. It wants compliance and does not want embarrassment. It will not hurry or extend a deadline because Disney was arguing internally.

### The Raider

The Raider holds nothing Disney needs. It is an investor seeking board seats and a breakup of the company. There is no route in; it approaches Disney, its Board, the press and individual executives. Its weapon is offering people a better arrangement than they believe they currently receive. It gives nothing before obtaining what it wants.

## Ambitions, rivals and the Finale

Each office wants one verifiable outcome at the Finale and races one rival over the same visible object. These ambitions are openly printed so other people can make offers against them.

| Office | Public Finale ambition | Rival and contested object |
|---|---|---|
| D'Amaro | Initial two of the three major company bets | Walden; the three bets |
| Walden | Stop two projects she did not sponsor and protect two new ones with named owners | D'Amaro; the three bets |
| Johnston | Finish with Cash at 5 or higher | Pitaro; the sports-rights ceiling |
| Bergman | Lock four dates while leaving two windows open | Feige; the premium windows |
| Mazloum | Keep two physical projects funded, including one partner-funded project | Smith; the physical-versus-platform tally |
| Pitaro | Close one rights package above the ceiling while retaining a substitute | Johnston; the sports-rights ceiling |
| Feige | Keep three Marvel projects alive, including one led by someone other than a returning star | Bergman; the premium windows |
| Smith | Launch one shared platform that survives a live disaster with no more than two exceptions | Mazloum; the physical-versus-platform tally |
| Gutierrez | Turn three conflicts into written third routes and close at least two | O'Connell; the News item |
| O'Connell | Record two Cash improvements while keeping all three named capabilities | Gutierrez; the News item |

The five direct races are D'Amaro–Walden, Johnston–Pitaro, Bergman–Feige, Mazloum–Smith and Gutierrez–O'Connell. Someone resisting a sensible plan may be protecting an ambition whose contested object you have not noticed.

### The spike

Resolved Directives are stacked chronologically on the dais spike with their outcomes. The spike records who led, signed, refused, withdrew and succeeded. It is public. If you want evidence of whether somebody delivers, inspect the record rather than relying on reputation.

### The Finale

After the final Monthly Close on Sunday, the dais reads the endings from the spike and the visible state of the table. Delegates compare their ambitions with initials, marks, windows, dials, the News item and the tally.

A Monthly Close happens ten times and reports one simulated month. The **Finale** happens once and determines the ending. They are different events.

## When you are stuck

### Someone asks for your signature

Read the price of your yes before negotiating. Ask for the price first. If they meet it, decide whether the risk is worth taking. If they do not, refuse.

### You want something but cannot pay

Offer a specific Promise Note. Find the dependency you supply and trade it. Or ask what the other person wants, find the person who owns it and broker a three-way deal.

### You do not understand the discussion

Ask, “What number is that based on?” If the answer remains unclear, ask who outside the room has agreed and what happens if the proposal fails.

### Someone pressures you to agree quickly

Ask, “What happens if it does not work?” Then read the printed default. Speed is often how somebody tries to obtain a signature you would refuse after examining the downside.

### You have no working cards

Ask another delegate to spend one card repairing you and negotiate what you will provide in return. Their repair costs one; self-repair costs two. If nobody repairs you, the normal recovery rules still apply at the next Close and comses opening. Meanwhile you continue negotiating, and everyone who requires your consent must wait.

### Someone overrode you

The damage is real and public. Find a colleague to repair you. Remember that the person who bypassed your price is likely to need your office again.

### You have been silent

Read one “what only I know” fact from your Office Brief. Or use one of the four questions. Both moves are designed to let you re-enter without inventing a speech.

### Somebody broke a verbal promise

State it once, publicly and precisely: “You told me you would do X. Are you doing X?” A verbal promise is not binding, but the room can update how much it trusts that person's future words.

### Your plan needs somebody outside the room

Name that person on the Directive, then check the relevant route in [the outside-office section](#the-ten-outside-offices). Your immediate negotiation is usually with the colleague who controls access.

### You believe doing nothing is correct

Say so and point to the default. Explain why accepting that consequence is preferable to the available actions. Deliberate inaction is a decision; silent paralysis is not.

## What good play looks like

Know three things about your office: what you control, what you lack and who holds it. Walk towards those people. Say the price of your yes aloud. Use the four questions on people who sound more confident than you. File imperfect Directives instead of mistaking conversation for progress.

Your first attempts will be wrong. A Directive will be returned. You may sign something too cheaply. You may resent somebody who was simply protecting their function. Those are not departures from the game; they are how the game teaches itself.

Remember the first sentence: **nobody in this room can finish anything alone. That is not the obstacle. That is the game.**
