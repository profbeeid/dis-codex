**The Walt Disney Company Crisis Committee**  
   
**A note to the incoming leadership**  
   
We are joining the leadership of the most valuable storytelling company in human history at the moment and it is quietly going out of style. When we read the financials, everything looks fine. The company is profitable, earnings are growing, we just reclaimed the box-office crown for the year, we are buying back billions in our own stock. By every number Wall Street watches this quarter, Disney is healthy.  
   
Now look at a ten-year-old. She does not want to be a princess; she wants to be a YouTuber. Her favorite "character" is a real person who looks into a camera and talks to her by name. She builds her own worlds in a game we do not own. She has never sat through one of our films without a second screen in her hand, and she thinks a twenty-two-minute story is slow.  
   
That is the crisis. Everyone knows that Disney wither the quarter while losing the 21st century. The machine that made Disney powerful assumed that the whole world would grow up loving the same handful of stories, and that we would own those stories. That assumption is dissolving in real time. Everything else on our agenda (the streaming losses, the government coming after our licenses, the fights with the cable carriers, the anger at our own ticket prices) is a symptom of the same disease.  
   
Our job is to decide whether Disney still matters in twenty years, and to do it while the market, the government, our own stars, and each other are pulling in opposite directions. Choose our loyalties carefully. Not everyone in the room wants the same company to survive.

 

**Definitions**

* **Monoculture**, a shared culture where most people consume the same few stories at the same time. Disney's historical fuel.  
* **Algorithmic fragmentation**, the personalization of media into individual feeds, dissolving shared culture into niches.  
* **The flywheel**, Disney's self-reinforcing loop: character → film → park → merchandise → back to character. Depends on children imprinting on owned characters.  
* **Parasocial bond**, a one-sided sense of personal relationship with a creator or performer. The thing creators have and fictional characters increasingly don't.  
* **IP (Intellectual Property)**, owned characters and stories (Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar, the classics). Disney's core asset and, increasingly, its crutch.  
* **Franchise fatigue**, audience exhaustion from too many entries in the same universe.  
* **The bundle**, the old cable package many households paid for regardless of use; a monoculture cash machine now collapsing.  
* **DTC (direct-to-consumer)**, selling streaming straight to viewers (Disney+, Hulu, ESPN's app). Higher control, worse economics.  
* **Churn**, customers subscribing then quickly canceling; the disease of streaming.  
* **Linear**, traditional scheduled TV (cable and broadcast networks); the declining old world.  
* **Carriage dispute**, a fight between a channel owner (Disney) and a distributor over fees; can end in a blackout.  
* **Cord-cutting**, households dropping cable for streaming.  
* **Broadcast license**, government permission for a TV station to exist; the leverage the regulator now holds.  
* **Activist investor**, a shareholder who buys in to force change (often a breakup) and pop the stock.  
* **Buyback**, a company spending cash to repurchase its own shares; boosts near-term per-share numbers, spends money that could fund long-term bets.

   
   
 

 

**Chapter 1**  
   
**A brief history of Disney**  
We are about to read the history of a company that nearly died at least four times. The five episodes that follow are selected because each one reveals a piece of Disney's DNA that we will need in the committee room. Read them in order. The ending of each paarts creates the conditions for the next crisis.  
   
**1\. Snow White and the Invention of Emotional Risk (1934–1938)**  
The year is 1934\. Walt Disney is thirty-two years old, and he is furious about the future.  
His studio produces animated shorts — seven-minute cartoons, played before feature films in theaters, starring a mouse who had already made Disney more famous than most Hollywood executives. The Silly Symphonies series had won Academy Awards. Mickey Mouse appeared in newspapers, on cereal boxes, on children's wristwatches. By the commercial standards of the day, the Disney Brothers Cartoon Studio was a success.  
   
But Walt understood something his brother Roy, his bankers, and the entire Hollywood establishment did not: the short cartoon was a dead end. No matter how good a seven-minute short was, it would never command its own ticket price, never generate enough revenue to build an enduring institution, never allow Disney to tell the kind of stories he actually wanted to tell. The shorts were appetizers. Walt wanted to cook the main course.  
   
In June 1934, he announced to the *New York Times* that he intended to produce a full-length animated feature film — something no American studio had ever attempted. He chose *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs*, a Brothers Grimm fairy tale he had first encountered as a teenager watching a silent film in Kansas City. The initial budget was $250,000, already ten times the cost of a typical Silly Symphony.  
What happened next is one of the most instructive episodes in corporate history, and it is almost always taught wrong.  
   
**The institutional bet**  
The standard telling goes like this: Walt Disney was a genius, he bet everything on Snow White, it worked, the end. This version is useless. It teaches we nothing except "be lucky."  
The real story is about what kind of bet Walt was making and who had to bear its consequences.  
The production dragged on for three years. Costs climbed past $500,000, past $750,000, past $1 million. Walt demanded retakes. Entire animated sequences — fully completed, costing months of labor — were cut because they slowed the emotional rhythm of the story. The animator Ward Kimball saw two of his finished sequences deleted; he was so demoralized he nearly quit the studio. Walt kept him by offering him a lead role on the next film. This was a management technique Walt would use for the rest of his life: sacrificing the present work to protect the future worker.  
   
By mid-production, the studio had run through its cash. Walt mortgaged his house. Roy went to the Bank of America to beg for more credit. The banker, Joseph Rosenberg, sat through a rough screening of the unfinished film without expression. When the lights came up, he turned to the Disney brothers and said, reportedly: "That thing is going to make a hatful of money." The loan was approved.  
Hollywood, meanwhile, had its own term for the project: "Disney's Folly." The industry consensus was that audiences could tolerate animation for seven minutes but would never sit through eighty. The bright colors would give people headaches. Children would get bored. Adults would feel ridiculous. Walt's own wife Lillian tried to talk him out of it. So did Roy.  
 

**What was actually being invented**  
Here is the question that separates a surface-level reading of this story from a useful one: *was Snow White a longer cartoon, or a new medium?* If it was merely a longer cartoon, the cost was absurd. We do not mortgage our house to make a bigger version of something that already exists. But Walt was not making a bigger cartoon. He was testing a proposition that, if true, would change the economics of storytelling permanently: *audiences can form genuine emotional attachments to animated characters, not merely laugh at them, but fear for them, grieve with them, hope for them.*  
   
This distinction matters because it explains everything Disney would become. The Mickey Mouse shorts made people laugh. Snow White made people cry. The distance between those two reactions is the distance between a novelty business and a century-long empire. Laughter is transactional. Emotional attachment is an asset that compounds. When Snow White premiered at the Carthay Circle Theatre on December 21, 1937, the audience included Charlie Chaplin, Shirley Temple, Clark Gable, and Marlene Dietrich — many of the same people who had spent three years calling the project a folly. The film reportedly moved several of them to tears. Six days later, Walt and the seven dwarfs appeared on the cover of *Time*.  
   
The numbers confirmed what the tears had already demonstrated. Snow White earned $8 million in its initial release against its $1.5 million cost, briefly becoming the highest-grossing sound film in history until *Gone with the Wind* surpassed it a year later. Merchandise sales added another $8 million. The profits built Disney's new Burbank studio. The first animated soundtrack album in history was released. A new category of cinema had been created, not by technology, but by the audacity of treating animation as a vessel for serious human emotion.  
   
Disney's greatest bets are never bets on technology. They are bets on the depth of emotional connection a new form can create. Snow White succeeded because a princess, drawn in ink and paint by five hundred artists using 1,500 shades of color, made a theater full of grown adults feel something real. The object of the wager was not the medium. It was the audience's capacity for love. The question is never "do we have the technology?" The question is always "can we make them love it?"  
   
The German expressionist films *Nosferatu* and *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari*, which Walt personally recommended to his staff during production, shaped the visual grammar of the Queen's transformation and Snow White's flight through the forest. This is worth knowing: Disney's first masterpiece drew its darkness from European avant-garde cinema, not from the wholesome Americana the brand would later come to represent. The DNA was never as innocent as the marketing suggested.  
   
**2\. Television, Disneyland, and the Art of Turning an Enemy into Fuel (1953–1960)**  
Snow White financed a studio. But one masterpiece does not guarantee a second. The films that followed — *Pinocchio*, *Fantasia*, *Bambi* — were artistic triumphs and commercial disappointments, their international revenues destroyed by World War II. Disney survived the 1940s by making military training films for the U.S. government. By the early 1950s, the studio was profitable again but structurally limited: it made films, it licensed characters, and it worried about the future. That future had a name, and it terrified the entire motion picture industry: television.  
   
**The enemy at the gate**  
Between 1948 and 1955, the number of American households with a television set went from roughly one million to thirty-five million. Movie theater attendance collapsed. Studios that had dominated American culture for three decades watched their audiences migrate to a small screen in the living room. The industry's response was almost uniformly defensive. Studios withheld their libraries from television. They invested in widescreen formats, 3-D, and spectacle — anything to make the theater experience feel irreplaceable. Television was the enemy. The correct strategy, as far as most studio executives were concerned, was to build higher walls.  
   
Walt Disney looked at the same situation and saw something entirely different. He had a problem. Since at least 1948, he had been sketching plans for a new kind of amusement park — clean, immersive, thematically organized around storytelling rather than thrill rides. He called it Disneyland. Every expert told him it was a terrible idea. Amusement parks were dirty, seasonal, low-margin businesses associated with carnival barkers and rigged games. No park without a roller coaster had ever succeeded commercially. Banks refused to finance it. His own board of directors was skeptical.  
   
Walt needed three things: money to build the park, attention to fill it on opening day, and a way to keep Disney characters present in American homes between film releases. Television, the medium that was destroying cinema, could provide all three.  
   
   
**The deal**  
In 1953, Walt approached all three television networks — CBS, NBC, and ABC. He offered them a weekly television program produced by Disney. But there was a condition: whichever network got the show would also invest in Disneyland. CBS and NBC wanted the programming but refused to gamble on the park. ABC, the weakest of the three networks, was desperate enough to say yes.  
   
The deal, signed on April 2, 1954, was structured with a precision that deserves study: ABC invested $500,000 directly in Disneyland, Inc. and guaranteed an additional $4.5 million in bank loans. In exchange, ABC received a 34.48% ownership stake in the park, the rights to a weekly Disney television program, first refusal on all future Disney television projects, and all profits from the park's food concessions for ten years. The concessions clause reflected ABC-Paramount's experience in movie theaters, where popcorn and candy often generated more profit than ticket sales.  
   
Notice what Walt had constructed. This was not a simple transaction in which one party paid another for a service. Each party's contribution *altered the probability that the other's business would succeed*: ABC's investment and loan guarantee made Disneyland financially possible. Disney's television program gave ABC the distinctive programming it needed to compete with CBS and NBC — in the 1953-54 season, ABC had zero shows in the top thirty; after *Disneyland* premiered on October 27, 1954, the show became the sixth-highest-rated program on television, and ABC attracted nearly half its advertising billings from Disney alone. The television program, in turn, served as a weekly promotional vehicle for the park that had not yet opened, building anticipation in millions of American living rooms. And the park, once operational, would generate new stories, new characters, and new footage that could feed back into the television program.  
Walt had not merely entered television. He had woven television, cinema, a physical place, and consumer products into a single self-reinforcing system.  
   
**The synergy map**  
In 1957, Walt sketched a diagram — the famous "Synergy Map," drawn on what legend insists was a napkin — that made the system explicit. At the center sat Walt Disney Studios, the creative engine that generated characters and stories. Radiating outward were television, theme parks, merchandise, music, and publications. The arrows between them flowed in both directions. Each division promoted every other division. A film created characters. Characters drove park attendance. Park visits deepened affection. Affection sold merchandise. Merchandise kept the characters present in homes between films. And the cycle began again.  
   
Todd Zenger, writing in the *Harvard Business Review* in 2013, called this map "a corporate theory" — not merely a strategy but a statement about how value is created, captured, and circulated across an integrated system. The Reforge blog, in a widely circulated analysis, argued that the 1957 map "still represents a fairly accurate picture of modern-day Disney" more than sixty years later. This is both a testament to Walt's foresight and, as we will see in later chapters, a quiet warning: when a company's strategy has not fundamentally changed in six decades, the question is whether it is timeless or whether it is merely old.  
   
**The buyback**  
Here is the part of the Disneyland-ABC story that business school summaries tend to skip: Walt always intended to buy ABC out. The partnership was designed as temporary dependency in exchange for permanent strategic control. Once the park was proven — once the loans were repaid, the revenue was flowing, and the concept had been validated — Disney repurchased ABC's stake. In 1960, Roy Disney paid $7.5 million in cash plus a five-year share of concessions to buy back the 34.48% ABC had acquired for $500,000 six years earlier. ABC made roughly fifteen times its money. Disney recovered full ownership of its most important physical asset.  
   
This pattern — use a partner's capital and distribution to build something, then reacquire control once the asset is proven — would recur throughout Disney history. It is the pattern Iger followed when he repaired the Pixar relationship in 2006\. It is the pattern Disney attempted, less successfully, with the Fox acquisition in 2019\. And it is the pattern the committee should be thinking about when it considers Disney's relationship with platforms like YouTube, Epic Games, and Roblox today.  
   
When a new medium threatens Disney's existing business, the instinct should not be to resist it. The instinct should be to ask: what can we build by riding it? Walt did not treat television as a replacement for cinema. He treated it as attention infrastructure — a way to insert Disney into the daily rhythms of American life, not just the occasional trip to the movie theater. He accepted a temporary loss of control (ABC's ownership stake, ABC's scheduling authority) in order to gain a permanent strategic position (the park, the weekly television presence, the flywheel).  
   
The most dangerous word in the committee room is "defend." When a delegate says "we must defend our content from TikTok" or "we must protect theatrical releases from streaming," ask ourselves to remember this episode. Walt's first instinct, when confronted with an existential threat, was not to defend. It was to build something new that the threat itself would help finance.  
   
**3\. The Renaissance: How Magic Becomes Money, and When Money Begins to Kill Magic (1984–1999)**  
Walt Disney died on December 15, 1966\. He was sixty-five. His brother Roy died five years later. What followed was a long, slow institutional paralysis that nearly destroyed the company.  
   
**The lost years**  
For almost two decades, the studio was run by men who had worked under Walt and who governed by a single, paralyzing question: *What would Walt do?* The answer, inevitably, was to repeat what Walt had already done. The animation department produced films that looked backward (*The Aristocats*, *Robin Hood*, *The Fox and the Hound*) technically competent but emotionally cautious. The theme parks coasted on rides Walt had designed. The company's most valuable assets — its library, its characters, its land, its brand — sat underutilized. As the cultural critic Richard Schickel observed in *The Disney Version* (1968, revised 1985), the organization had calcified into a museum of its founder's taste rather than a living continuation of his creative method.  
   
By the early 1980s, the gap between Disney's asset value and its market performance had become large enough to attract the attention of corporate raiders. Saul Steinberg, the financier, launched a hostile takeover attempt in 1984\. His thesis was straightforward: the company's parts were worth more broken up and sold than they were being managed as a whole. The land alone was worth billions. The film library was undermonetized. The parks were underpriced. Management was coasting.  
   
Disney survived the Steinberg raid through greenmail (essentially paying him to go away) but the brush with corporate death forced the board to act. Roy E. Disney, Walt's nephew, and Sid Bass, the company's largest shareholder, recruited new leadership from outside.  
   
**The Eisner arrival**  
In September 1984, Michael Eisner became CEO. He brought with him Frank Wells as president and Jeffrey Katzenberg as chairman of the Walt Disney Studios. All three came from the traditional film industry — Eisner and Katzenberg from Paramount, Wells from Warner Bros. None of them had particular expertise in animation.  
   
This is one of the great ironies of the Disney Renaissance: the creative revival that saved the company was led by executives who initially considered shutting down the animation department altogether. As the animation scholar Christopher Holifield noted in a 2023 essay for the academic journal *Fantasy/Animation*, "Animation was far from Eisner's priority; in fact, he and his production chief Jeffrey Katzenberg initially considered outsourcing or even shutting it down altogether." Eisner's early wins at Disney were in live-action comedy, television production, and aggressive monetization of existing assets, raising theme park ticket prices, selling classic films on VHS cassettes, licensing characters more aggressively.  
   
The animation department, meanwhile, had been exiled. In 1985, to make room for live-action production on the main Burbank lot, the animators were physically moved to warehouses and trailers two miles away in Glendale. This exile, intended as temporary, lasted a decade. The documentary *Waking Sleeping Beauty* (2009), directed by Disney producer Don Hahn, captures the emotional texture of this period: talented artists working in dingy spaces, uncertain whether the company that bore Walt Disney's name still cared about the art form Walt Disney had invented.  
   
**The spark**  
The creative turning point was not a single decision but a convergence. The success of *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* in 1988 — a Touchstone Pictures co-production with Amblin Entertainment that mixed live action and animation to spectacular commercial and critical effect — demonstrated that audiences still had an appetite for animated storytelling. *Oliver & Company* (1988), a modest animated feature, outgrossed Don Bluth's *The Land Before Time* at the box office, proving that Disney's brand still carried weight in the marketplace.  
   
But the real catalyst was the arrival of two Broadway songwriters named Howard Ashman and Alan Menken. Ashman and Menken had created the Off-Broadway musical *Little Shop of Horrors*. They understood something that the Disney animation department had forgotten and that Katzenberg, despite his considerable commercial instincts, had never fully grasped: in a musical, songs are not decoration. Songs are structure. A character singing about what they want in the first act is not an interlude — it is the *engine* of the story. It tells the audience who this person is, what they desire, and what they are willing to risk, in three minutes of melody.  
   
When Katzenberg first heard Ariel's ballad "Part of Our World" in *The Little Mermaid*, he wanted to cut it. He thought children would get bored during a slow song. The directors, John Musker and Ron Clements, pushed back. They reminded Katzenberg that Snow White's "Someday My Prince Will Come" had served the same structural function fifty years earlier — and that the bosses at MGM had once wanted to cut "Over the Rainbow" from *The Wizard of Oz* before being overruled by people with better instincts. The song stayed.  
*The Little Mermaid* opened on November 17, 1989\. Roger Ebert wrote in his review: "Here at last, once again, is the kind of liberating, original, joyful Disney animation that we all remember from *Snow White*, *Pinocchio*, and the other first-generation classics." The film earned $84 million domestically. It won two Academy Awards. It sold two million copies of its soundtrack album within a year.  
   
What followed was extraordinary. *Beauty and the Beast* (1991) became the first animated film nominated for the Academy Award for Best Picture. *Aladdin* (1992), powered by Robin Williams's volcanic performance as the Genie, grossed over $500 million worldwide and fundamentally changed the practice of celebrity voice casting in animation. *The Lion King* (1994) earned $968 million globally, a record for animation that would stand for years.  
   
The director Kirk Wise later said of Howard Ashman, who died of AIDS in March 1991 before seeing the completed *Beauty and the Beast*: "He was the one person responsible for the Disney Renaissance." This is both a tribute and, if we think about it carefully, an indictment — because it means the entire revival rested on the creative vision of one man who could not be replaced, working inside a corporate structure that had not known it needed him until he arrived.  
   
**The monetization architecture**  
The Renaissance films did not just succeed at the box office. They became the nucleus of a monetization system more sophisticated than anything Walt had built. Each film was surrounded by a commercial architecture designed to extract value across decades: The theatrical release established the emotional bond. The home video release, managed through the "Disney Vault" — a deliberate scarcity strategy in which films were available for purchase only during limited windows before being "locked away" — created urgency and maintained premium pricing. Consumer products (toys, clothing, school supplies) kept the characters present in daily life between film releases. Broadway adaptations extended the stories into a new medium and a new revenue stream — *Beauty and the Beast* opened on Broadway in 1994, followed by *The Lion King* in 1997, which became one of the highest-grossing theatrical productions in history. Theme park attractions turned the two-dimensional stories into three-dimensional physical experiences. Television spin-offs and direct-to-video sequels reached audiences who could not afford the theater or the park.  
   
This system was the flywheel Walt had sketched on his napkin in 1957, now operating at industrial scale. And it worked magnificently, for a time.  
   
   
   
**The extraction threshold**  
Here is where the Renaissance story becomes essential for the committee, because it contains the seed of every crisis Disney faces today. At what point does monetization stop deepening affection and begin consuming it?  
   
The question is not theoretical. By the mid-1990s, Disney was producing direct-to-video sequels to its Renaissance films — *The Return of Jafar*, *The Lion King II*, *The Little Mermaid II* — that were transparently cheaper, artistically diminished versions of the originals. The consumer products division was licensing Disney characters so aggressively that they appeared on everything from toothbrushes to breakfast cereals to automobile air fresheners. The characters became ubiquitous — and ubiquity, for a brand built on magic, is the opposite of magic.  
   
Katzenberg himself recognized this dynamic. In a now-famous 1991 internal memo (later leaked), he argued that Disney needed to keep production costs under control and avoid the assumption that bigger budgets automatically produced better films. The memo was commercially shrewd but creatively ominous: it treated films primarily as inputs to the merchandising machine rather than as ends in themselves. After *The Lion King*, the internal dynamics at Disney shifted — increasingly, the question asked of new projects was not "is this story worth telling?" but "how many products can this story generate?"  
   
Katzenberg left Disney in 1994, in a bitter dispute with Eisner over credit, compensation, and succession. He co-founded DreamWorks SKG with Steven Spielberg and David Geffen, taking with him an intimate understanding of Disney's creative process that he would use to build a direct competitor. The departure of Katzenberg is one of those moments where personal ego and institutional health collide with devastating consequences: Disney lost the executive most responsible for its creative revival because the CEO could not share credit.  
   
At its best, Disney does not choose between magic and money. It creates the emotional source first, then surrounds it with a commercial architecture that collects value for decades. But the order is not negotiable. The moment the commercial architecture begins dictating what stories get told, the flywheel reverses: each new product depletes affection rather than deepening it.  
   
The Renaissance teaches we to watch for a specific symptom: when divisions inside Disney start imposing commercial requirements on stories *before the stories exist* (when the theme park team demands a ride-friendly set piece, when the consumer products team demands a merchandisable sidekick, when the streaming team demands a releasable franchise extension on a quarterly schedule) the creative engine is no longer at the center of the flywheel. It has been displaced by the extraction apparatus.  
   
We will see this symptom everywhere in the 2026 crisis. The live-action remakes. The franchise sequels. The content volume on Disney+. These are not failures of individual films. They are symptoms of a flywheel that has reversed its polarity.  
   
**4\. Save Disney: Who Owns the Soul of an Institution? (2003–2005)**  
Michael Eisner saved Disney in 1984\. By 2003, he was the reason it needed saving again. This is the episode about governance. Governance determines who gets to define what Disney is, and what happens when the person with legal authority has lost the moral right to speak for the institution.  
   
**The decay**  
Eisner's first decade at Disney was genuinely transformative. He revitalized the animation division, expanded the theme parks, launched the Disney Channel, built a successful television production business, and increased the company's market capitalization from $2 billion to over $80 billion. He was celebrated as one of the most successful CEOs in American corporate history. His second decade was corrosive.  
   
Frank Wells, the president who had balanced Eisner's creative ambition with operational discipline and institutional humility, died in a helicopter accident in 1994\. After Wells's death, Eisner consolidated power. He pushed out Katzenberg. He clashed with Michael Ovitz, the superagent he had recruited as president, who lasted fourteen months before being fired with a severance package exceeding $100 million. He alienated Steve Jobs, whose Pixar studio had produced *Toy Story*, *A Bug's Life*, *Monsters, Inc.*, and *Finding Nemo* under a distribution deal with Disney — films that were keeping Disney relevant in animation while the internal studio floundered. The Pixar negotiations broke down. Jobs publicly announced that Pixar would not renew its Disney partnership.  
   
Inside the company, a culture of fear had developed. Talented executives left or were pushed out. Creative risk-taking diminished. The animation department, which had generated the Renaissance, produced a series of commercial and artistic disappointments — *Atlantis*, *Treasure Planet*, *Brother Bear*, *Home on the Range*. The films were not catastrophes, but they were not the kind of work that plants love in a child. They were institutional product, made by a committee, for a schedule.  
   
The board of directors, which should have served as a check on Eisner's consolidation of power, instead became a rubber stamp. Board members included Eisner's personal friends, his architect, and the principal of his children's former elementary school. Institutional Shareholder Services, the leading proxy advisory firm, would later rate Disney's board governance among the worst in the S\&P 500\.  
   
**The rebellion**  
In November 2003, Roy E. Disney (Walt's nephew, the last Disney family member on the board) resigned his directorship. He was joined by Stanley Gold, a board member and longtime Roy ally. Together, they launched a public campaign called "Save Disney."  
   
The campaign used the most powerful weapon available: the Disney name itself. Roy framed his rebellion not as a shareholder dispute but as a fight for the company's identity. In his resignation letter, he accused Eisner of transforming Disney into a "rapacious, soul-less" company that had abandoned the creative values that defined it. He launched a website (savedisney.com) and began publicly courting institutional shareholders.  
   
The question the Save Disney campaign raised is one the committee should argue about directly: *was Roy exercising legitimate moral authority, or was he exploiting an unelected hereditary privilege to override corporate governance?*  
   
There is no clean answer. Roy had no operational authority. He had not run a Disney division. His claim to leadership rested entirely on his surname and on the mythology of the company his uncle had founded. But his diagnosis of the problem was, by most accounts, accurate: the creative engine was stalling, Eisner's management style had become destructive, and the board was too compromised to intervene. Roy was wielding symbolic power — the power of the Disney name — against an incumbent who possessed legal power but had lost something harder to quantify: the trust of the people who made the company's products.  
   
**The predator**  
Crises compound. In February 2004, while the Save Disney campaign was eroding Eisner's internal legitimacy, Comcast (then America's largest cable operator) launched a hostile takeover bid worth $54 billion in stock.  
   
The timing was not coincidental. A Disney board member, Judith Estrin, publicly acknowledged what was happening: "There is a perception of weakness," she said during an investor conference call. "There is a lot of innuendo out there that is being repeated that is not reality." But perception, in corporate governance, creates its own reality. Internal weakness lowers the cost of external attack. External attack strengthens the argument of the internal rebels. Each crisis feeds the other.  
   
Comcast wanted ESPN, the ABC network, and the Disney content library. Brian Roberts, Comcast's CEO, saw a company whose assets were worth more than its current leadership could extract from them — precisely the same logic that had motivated Saul Steinberg's raid twenty years earlier. The cycle had repeated. Disney had been saved from a raider, rebuilt by new management, weakened by that management's eventual failures, and was now vulnerable to the next raider.  
   
The Comcast bid also forced a question the board could not avoid: *can Eisner credibly lead the defense of Disney when his own legitimacy is the thing making the company vulnerable?*  
   
**The shareholder confrontation**  
On March 3, 2004, at the annual shareholder meeting in Philadelphia, Roy Disney and Stanley Gold made their case to thousands of shareholders. They received a standing ovation. Eisner and Bob Iger, then Disney's president, answered an aggressive Q\&A.  
   
When the votes were counted, 43% of shareholders withheld their support from Eisner's reelection to the board. He was running unopposed. A withheld vote is not the same as a vote against — technically, Eisner retained his seat. But 43% is an astonishing number. It is a number that says: *we still have the title, but nearly half the people who own this company do not believe we should have it.* Institutional Shareholder Services had backed Roy and Gold's campaign, advising its clients to withhold support.  
   
The board, now unable to ignore the signal, stripped Eisner of his chairmanship while allowing him to remain as CEO — a compromise that satisfied nobody. Comcast, unable to convince Disney's board to negotiate and facing its own shareholders' skepticism (Comcast's stock was falling as the bid consumed executive attention), formally withdrew its offer in April 2004\.  
   
Eisner would announce his retirement in September 2005\. Bob Iger, his handpicked successor — and a man who had been Eisner's loyal subordinate throughout the turmoil — became CEO. One of Iger's first acts was to repair the Pixar relationship by acquiring the company outright for $7.4 billion, making Steve Jobs Disney's largest individual shareholder and installing Pixar's creative chief, John Lasseter, as the head of Disney Animation.  
   
Disney's founder mythology operates as an unwritten constitution. A leader can possess complete formal authority and still lose the right to speak for Disney. When creative legitimacy, board confidence, and shareholder confidence separate, predators arrive.  
   
Episode four teaches we three things we will need in the committee room: First, that governance is not a bureaucratic formality — it is a survival mechanism. The board's failure to maintain independence from Eisner directly created the conditions for both the Save Disney rebellion and the Comcast bid. Second, that institutional legitimacy has multiple currencies. Eisner had legal power. Roy had symbolic power. Comcast had financial power. The shareholders had voting power. None of these currencies is automatically dominant. The outcome depends on who deploys theirs most effectively at the right moment. Third, that the same qualities that make a leader successful in one era can make them destructive in the next. Eisner's aggression, commercial instinct, and willingness to override consensus were assets in 1984, when Disney was a sleepy company that needed to be shaken awake. By 2003, those same qualities had calcified into an inability to listen, an intolerance for dissent, and a refusal to plan for succession. The institution needed someone who could hold its accumulated power *lightly*. Eisner held it like a fist.  
   
**5\. Disney+, COVID, Chapek, and the Return of Iger: When Everything Happens at Once (2019–2022)**  
The four parts we have just read each centered on one dramatic question. This episode is different. It is a pile-up: multiple individually rational decisions producing one institutionally incoherent company. It should feel messier than the earlier chapters, because it is. The mess is the lesson.  
   
**The streaming wager**  
By 2018, Bob Iger had assembled the most valuable collection of intellectual property in entertainment history. The Pixar acquisition (2006, $7.4 billion) had restored Disney's animation credibility. The Marvel acquisition (2009, $4 billion) had given Disney access to the most commercially successful film franchise in history. The Lucasfilm acquisition (2012, $4 billion) had brought Star Wars — arguably the most culturally durable science-fiction mythology of the twentieth century — into the flywheel.  
   
Iger had solved the problem that destroyed Eisner: the internal creative engine was weak, so he purchased external creative engines whose relevance had already been proven. This was brilliant. It was also, in a way the committee should examine carefully, a confession: Disney could no longer reliably generate new affection from scratch. It could only acquire properties that had already generated it.  
   
In 2017, Iger made one more acquisition: 21st Century Fox, for $71 billion. This was different in kind from the Pixar, Marvel, and Lucasfilm deals. Fox was not a single creative engine with a proven catalogue. It was a sprawling conglomerate — film studios, television networks, regional sports networks, international channels — whose integration would be complex and whose fit with the Disney brand was imperfect. The primary strategic rationale was not creative but distributional: Fox's content library would provide the volume Disney needed to launch a direct-to-consumer streaming service that could compete with Netflix.  
Disney+ launched on November 12, 2019\. Within twenty-four hours, it had ten million subscribers. Within sixteen months, it had over one hundred million. The numbers were staggering and, as we now know, misleading.  
   
**What Disney+ was (and what it was not)**  
The committee should force itself to answer a question that Disney's leadership never fully resolved: *what is Disney+ for?* It cannot be all of these things simultaneously: A profitable standalone business requires high prices and controlled content spending. A defensive response to Netflix requires speed and scale, which means low prices and heavy spending. A global customer-acquisition system requires maximizing subscribers regardless of profitability. A distribution channel for the Disney library requires curation and brand discipline. The new center of the whole corporation requires organizational restructuring that subordinates every other division.  
   
During the subscriber-growth phase, Disney+ was priced at $6.99 per month — well below Netflix, well below the production cost of the content it was streaming. This was a deliberate choice: sacrifice near-term profitability to build a subscriber base quickly enough to satisfy Wall Street's appetite for growth metrics. The streaming division lost over $4 billion in a single fiscal year. These losses were subsidized by the theme parks division, which was generating record profits before COVID hit.  
   
The deeper problem was not the losses themselves but what the growth-at-all-costs strategy did to Disney's creative discipline. To fill the streaming library, Disney needed volume. Volume meant producing more Marvel shows, more Star Wars shows, more sequels and spinoffs and franchise extensions than the market could emotionally absorb. The flywheel, which depends on scarcity and love, was being asked to operate under conditions of abundance and obligation.  
   
**COVID**  
On March 12, 2020, Disney closed its theme parks worldwide. The parks division — the single most profitable cash engine in the company, the physical embodiment of the flywheel, the place where emotional attachment is converted into revenue at the highest margin — went dark.  
   
The financial impact was immediate and severe. In fiscal year 2020, the parks division lost $2.6 billion. Film releases were disrupted. *Mulan*, a $200 million live-action remake intended for theatrical distribution, was diverted to Disney+ as a premium-access title. The decision satisfied nobody: theatrical exhibitors felt betrayed, subscribers resented the additional charge, and the film's cultural reception was complicated by controversies unrelated to its artistic quality.  
   
But the streaming numbers surged. With millions of people locked in their homes, Disney+ subscriber growth accelerated dramatically. This created a dangerous feedback loop: the crisis made streaming look more essential than it was, which justified more spending on streaming content, which pulled creative resources away from the theatrical and parks experiences that had historically generated the deepest emotional connections.  
   
**The succession trap**  
In February 2020, Bob Iger stepped down as CEO and was succeeded by Bob Chapek, a longtime Disney executive whose background was in consumer products and theme parks. The transition was handled in a way that virtually guaranteed failure. Iger retained the title of Executive Chairman and continued to make major creative decisions. Chapek held the CEO title but not the full authority that comes with it. Employees, partners, and media executives understood that Iger remained the real center of gravity. When disagreements arose — and they arose constantly — the question was never "what does the CEO want?" but "what does Iger think?"  
   
This is a governance failure, and it is a specific kind: the predecessor who stays. A famous predecessor can stabilize a transition in the short term by reassuring employees and investors that continuity is preserved. But in the medium term, the predecessor's presence makes it impossible for the successor to establish independent authority. Every decision the successor makes is measured against what the predecessor would have done. The successor cannot develop a distinctive leadership identity because the organization is still oriented around someone else.  
   
Chapek compounded the structural problem with a series of decisions that alienated Disney's internal culture. He reorganized the company around a centralized distribution model that stripped creative executives of control over how their content reached audiences — a move that was administratively rational (it allowed Disney to optimize content allocation across theatrical, streaming, and home video) but culturally disastrous (it told filmmakers and showrunners that the distribution apparatus, not the creative vision, was now in charge). He raised theme park prices aggressively during the post-COVID reopening, implementing dynamic pricing and reservation systems that maximized revenue per visitor but eroded the egalitarian promise of the parks — the idea that Disneyland was a place everyone could afford to visit, not just the affluent.  
   
**The political collision**  
In early 2022, the Florida state legislature passed a bill restricting classroom instruction on sexual orientation and gender identity — colloquially known as the "Don't Say Gay" bill. Disney employees, particularly in the company's creative divisions, demanded that Disney publicly oppose the legislation. Chapek initially chose silence, then issued a series of statements that satisfied neither employees who wanted forceful opposition nor conservatives who wanted the company to stay out of politics.  
   
Florida's governor retaliated against Disney's eventual opposition by signing legislation to dissolve the Reedy Creek Improvement District — the special governmental jurisdiction, created in 1967, that had given Disney effective self-governance over its Florida resort property for fifty-five years. The dissolution (later modified into a state-controlled oversight board) threatened Disney's operational autonomy in its most profitable domestic location.  
   
The episode revealed something the committee should understand clearly: **Disney cannot be apolitical, because it has never been apolitical.** A company that tells children stories about right and wrong, that shapes values through narrative, that operates as a quasi-governmental entity within an American state, is making political choices every day — including the choice to remain silent, which is itself a political position. The question for the committee is not "should Disney take political stances?" The question is "which legitimacy is Disney willing to spend, and on what?"  
   
**Iger's return**  
On November 20, 2022, Disney's board fired Bob Chapek and reinstated Bob Iger as CEO. The move was announced on a Sunday evening. The board had concluded that Chapek had lost the confidence of employees, creative partners, and investors simultaneously. Iger's return was greeted with relief across the company and on Wall Street. Disney's stock price jumped. Employees expressed cautious optimism. The narrative — "the legendary CEO returns to save Disney" — was comforting and familiar.  
   
But the committee should resist comfort and ask harder questions. Bringing Iger back solved the immediate confidence crisis. It did not solve the structural problems that created the crisis: the streaming economics that do not yet work, the franchise fatigue that is eroding the creative engine, the succession question that Iger himself had failed to answer the first time. In a sense, Iger's return is the most Disneyan move possible — it is a sequel. And the Renaissance episode taught we what sequels usually mean for the flywheel.  
   
The deeper irony is that Iger, during his first tenure, is the person who made many of the decisions that created the current crisis. He authorized the Fox acquisition and its $71 billion in debt. He launched Disney+ and its growth-at-all-costs strategy. He chose Chapek as his successor. He designed the transition that failed. His return is not a correction of errors made by someone else. It is a correction of errors made by himself.  
Disney can survive a failed film. It can survive a bad quarter. It struggles when its businesses, leadership, brand, and public meaning begin telling different stories. This episode does not have a single clean lesson because the crisis does not have a single clean cause. What happened between 2019 and 2022 was the simultaneous breakdown of narrative coherence across every dimension of the company: the streaming strategy said "growth and volume," while the brand promise said "quality and scarcity." The parks strategy said "premium pricing for affluent guests," while the brand promise said "a place for everyone." The political stance said "we support our employees," while the institutional behavior said "we did not anticipate the consequences." The succession said "new leadership," while the organizational reality said "the old leader is still here."  
   
The committee's job is not to fix any one of these contradictions. It is to recognize that they are all expressions of the same underlying failure: Disney has lost the ability to tell itself a coherent story about what it is. And a company that cannot tell itself a coherent story is, by definition, a company that cannot tell one to the world.  
 

 

**Chapter 2**  
   
**The core crisis today is called “the end of the monoculture”**  
For a hundred years, Disney's power rested on a single fact about the world: culture was shared. A small number of stories, songs, and films reached almost everyone, at roughly the same time, and became the common language of a generation. Everyone knew Mickey. Everyone saw the same film in the same theater. Everyone watched the same channels bundled into the same cable package. This shared culture is called the monoculture, and Disney was built to manufacture it and to profit from it more efficiently than anyone alive.  
   
**Two children, one room**  
Picture two eight-year-olds on the same sofa. Same house, same afternoon, same brand of phone in each pair of hands. One is deep inside a Roblox world she built with a friend in another country. The other is watching a twenty-three-year-old open toys and scream about them to forty million strangers. Neither is watching anything the other is watching. Neither will ever, in their whole childhood, be handed the single story that their grandparents were handed, the film everyone saw, the song everyone knew, the mouse everyone loved.  
This is the scene every essay about the death of culture opens with, and it is meant to frighten we. Hold the fear for a second, then do the thing a good delegate always does: ask whether the scary story is even true. Because the entire Disney committee is balanced on top of one claim, that culture used to be *shared*, and now it is *shattered*, and that claim is far shakier, far stranger, and far more interesting than the study guide lets on.  
Let's take it apart, then decide what's left standing.  
   
**The dam, not the ocean**  
Start from first principles. What is a "monoculture"? People use the word to mean a time when a small number of stories, songs, and screens reached almost everyone at roughly the same moment, and became the shared language of a generation. The New Yorker's Kyle Chayka, in *Filterworld* (2024), traces how that shared world got replaced by billions of algorithmic feeds. The Tokyo-based writer W. David Marx, in *Status and Culture* (2022), describes the same dissolving of a common taste into private streams. This is the standard picture, and the committee assumes it.  
   
Here is the part the standard picture leaves out, and it flips the whole thing over.  
   
The monoculture was not the natural state of humanity. It was a costly man-made machine, and it ran for about fifty years.  
   
For almost all of human history, culture was *local*. A village had its songs; the next valley had different ones. Stories were oral, regional, particular. Nobody in 1750 shared a "generation-defining film," because there were no films, and there was no "everyone." What we nostalgically call the monoculture only became possible when a very specific set of technologies created a *bottleneck*: a handful of Hollywood studios, three American television networks, a limited number of radio frequencies, a record shop with only so much shelf space. When there are only three doors, everyone walks through the same three doors. That is not unity. That is a shortage of doors.  
So the internet did not smash a natural human oneness. It removed the shortage. The river that had been dammed into a single channel for fifty years is now running back into the thousand channels it always had. Chris Anderson called this "the long tail" back in 2006, the shift from a few giant hits to endless niches, once the shelf became infinite. Read that way, "the end of the monoculture" is not a fall from grace. It is a return to normal. The strange, unrepeatable thing was the middle of the twentieth century, and Disney was born, and grew fat, inside that strangeness.  
   
This matters for our strategy in a brutal way. If Disney's problem is *a few bad years of movies*, we fix it with better movies. If Disney's problem is that the specific historical accident that made Disney possible is now over, no movie fixes that, and every executive in the room who promises "we just need another hit" is either lying or hasn't understood the century they're standing in.  
   
**"Everyone" was always a smaller word than it sounds**  
Now the second crack in the founding idea, and this one should matter especially to a room full of Indonesian students. When Americans say "everyone shared the same culture," ask a sharp question: *everyone where?* A child in Surabaya in 1975 did not experience Disney the way a child in Ohio did. In Ohio, Disney was the air. In Surabaya, Disney was an *import*, an American myth arriving from outside, carrying American assumptions about beauty, destiny, romance, and success, wrapped in a mouse. The "global monoculture" always had a center and a periphery. It was shared the way a language is shared between a colonizer and a colony: unequally, and in one direction.  
   
We do not have to take my word for this. In 1971, in Salvador Allende's Chile, two writers, Ariel Dorfman and the Belgian sociologist Armand Mattelart, published *How to Read Donald Duck*, arguing that Disney comics were not innocent entertainment but a delivery system for capitalist and imperialist ideas, aimed straight at children in the Global South. The book was so threatening to the powerful that after the 1973 coup, Pinochet's soldiers reportedly burned copies and dumped them into the sea. Think about that. A regime felt it necessary to *drown a book about a cartoon duck.* Nobody burns a book about something harmless.  
I am not asking we to agree with Dorfman and Mattelart. Plenty of scholars find them heavy-handed, and reading a duck comic as a political weapon can tip into paranoia. But their core insight is unkillable and it is the anthropological heart of this whole committee: the stories we are given before we can reason become the water we swim in. We do not choose them. They choose we. Disney understood this better than any company in history, and that understanding, not any single film, is the real asset on the table.  
Which brings us to what Disney actually is.  
   
**The machine for manufacturing childhood**  
In 1957, Walt Disney sat down and drew a diagram the "Synergy Map." At the center: theatrical films and the creative talent of the studio. Radiating out, connected by arrows, little Mickeys running along each line: television, music, publications, merchandise licensing, and Disneyland. Every arm fed every other arm. Films made characters; television plugged the films; the park let we stand inside them; merchandise sent we home with them; and all of it drove we back to the next film. A Harvard professor, Todd Zenger, later called this kind of design "corporate theory," and business schools teach the napkin to this day. (A detail worth savoring: for the first five years that this genius diagram existed, the company still lost money. Even perfect machines take time to spin.)  
   
But notice what the machine is actually *for*. Strip away the arrows and the flywheel has exactly one job at its core, and everything else is plumbing: plant a durable emotional bond with a character, inside a human being, while that human is too young to defend against it. Every other stage, the park ticket, the plush toy, the live-action remake forty years later, is a way of collecting rent on a love that was installed in childhood.  
   
This is why Disney is not a normal company and why a Disney crisis is not a normal crisis. Neil Postman argued in *The Disappearance of Childhood* (1982) that "childhood" is not a biological fact but a thing that media *invents*, print created the wall between what children know and what adults know, and electronic media is now dissolving that wall. If Postman is right, then Disney was one of the great architects of the modern idea of childhood itself. It didn't just sell to children. It helped *build* the childhood it then sold to. That is why attacking Disney feels, to so many adults, like an attack on innocence, on their own younger selves, on something sacred. We are not managing a media firm and instead we are the current custodian of a machine that has been reaching into the pre-rational minds of the planet for ninety years. Hold that weight. It is the source of both Disney's power and the fury that greets its every stumble.  
   
**Two blades of one scissors**  
So: the monoculture was an anomaly, "everyone" was always a partial word, and Disney's real product is an emotional imprint on children. Now the algorithm arrives. What actually happens?  
The obvious answer, attention *fragments*, everyone floats off into a private feed, is only half of it, and the smaller half. Here is the twist that the sharpest critics have caught. Chayka's *Filterworld* argues that the same algorithms that split us apart also flatten us together. The feeds promise personalization but deliver homogenization: a billion private streams that all drift toward the same safe, frictionless, screenshot-ready mean. He first noticed it in 2016 writing about how coffee shops and Airbnbs from Nairobi to Portland had all started looking identical, he called it "AirSpace." The rule of this world, he says, is *go viral or die*. W. David Marx pushes the point further in *Blank Space* (2025): an age of reboots, sequels, and creative timidity, a culture that has stopped inventing and started reheating.  
   
So the founding idea of our committee is really two forces at once, cutting like the two blades of a single pair of scissors:

* The audience splinters into niches too small to build a mass myth around.  
* The stuff that gets made converges on the safe and the familiar, because that is what the algorithm rewards.

Disney is caught on both blades. It is pushed to keep pumping out safe franchise product to feed the machine, which burns out its own creativity, while the shared audience that once made those franchises valuable dissolves underneath it. This is the deep engine. The five loud crises we will spend sixteen hours fighting, the war for attention, the decay of quality, the streaming money-trap, the anger in the parks, the political siege, are not five separate fires. They are five *rooms* that this one draft blows through. Do not mistake a symptom for the disease. We will not open those rooms here; the guide does that. Here we are still deciding whether the disease is even what everyone says it is. Because it might not be.  
   
***Now argue the other side***  
A good analyst is their own harshest opponent. Let’s try to demolish everything above.  
   
*Counter-argument one: the monoculture didn't die, it just moved.* Look at the actual biggest shared cultural events of recent years. A Korean survival drama, *Squid Game*, became a planet-wide obsession. A Japanese animation industry that Hollywood ignored for decades is now global. K-pop groups fill stadiums on continents that have never spoken a word of Korean. A young man with a camera, MrBeast, commands an audience larger than most nations. None of them are Disney. So maybe shared culture is perfectly alive, maybe it is *more* global than the old American version ever was, and Disney's real problem is not that the campfire went out. It's that the campfire is now lit by a Korean studio, a Japanese animator, a twenty-six-year-old, and a recommendation engine owned in China. For Disney, a world with a monoculture *it does not own* is far more dangerous than a world with no monoculture at all. That is a colder, sharper diagnosis than "culture is dead," and we should prefer it, because it is more likely to be true.  
   
*Counter-argument two: maybe the whole lament is a status panic.* When *Foreign Policy* reviewed Marx's *Blank Space* in early 2026, it hit back hard: we have never been *further* from a monoculture, it argued, because we have never had more access to more art from more of the world. One critic put the knife in cleanly, that the doom-mongers are mistaking their own loss of status for a loss of standards. For a century, a small class of Western editors, critics, and studios decided what "everyone" would see. The internet fired those gatekeepers. Of course the ex-gatekeepers experience that as *decline*, their power to define the center is gone. But "I am no longer the one who chooses for everyone" is not the same statement as "culture is dying," even though it feels identical from the inside. Watch for this move all committee long: an executive describing the loss of *Disney's* centrality as the loss of *culture's* health. Those are different claims. Only one of them is our job to fix.  
   
*Counter-argument three: every old generation says this.* There was a moral panic that novels would rot young women's brains. Then comics. Then radio, then television, then video games, each in turn accused of stealing the children and ending civilization. "Kids don't imprint on our stuff anymore" is the oldest complaint in media, and it has been wrong every single time, the kids simply grew up and built the next establishment. And the data refuses to cooperate with the pure doom story, too. American children under twelve still watch *more* traditional television than any other age group under thirty-five, often sitting beside a parent or grandparent. Plenty of them are still streaming decades-old Disney shows right now. The flywheel is wobbling. It has not stopped.  
   
**One more thing, and this is a lesson in itself.** We may have seen a clean statistic floating around, something like "70% of people feel their media diet is uniquely their own." Treat numbers like that with suspicion. I could not trace it to a credible source, and a figure that round and that convenient is usually a marketing survey or an invention that got repeated until it sounded like fact. This is a committee, not a sermon: if we cite a number on the floor, be ready to say where it came from. A delegate who quotes a fake statistic with confidence has handed every opponent a free weapon.  
   
**Why the worry survives anyway**  
I have now spent three arguments trying to kill the committee's founding idea. Here is why it does not quite die, and this is the version of the crisis I actually believe. Grant all of it. Grant that the monoculture was an anomaly, that "everyone" was always partial, that culture merely moved, that every generation panics. One thing is still specifically, mechanically true: the exact thing Disney's flywheel needs at stage one is genuinely harder to get now, and its replacements do not spin the wheel.  
   
The bond a child forms today is increasingly with something Disney cannot own and cannot always even *narrate*. A Roblox world is not a story we receive; it is a place we *build*, the child is the author, not the audience. A YouTuber is not a princess frozen forever in one perfect film; he is a person who seems to *answer back*. Sociologists have a term for the one-directional intimacy people feel toward a screen figure, "parasocial interaction," named by Donald Horton and Richard Wohl all the way back in 1956\. Disney was the greatest parasocial engine ever built: we loved Cinderella and Cinderella never changed and never left. But the parasocial bond is now attaching to creators who feel like *friends who talk to we*, and to games where the child is the god of a world of their own making. That is a different kind of love. It may be a deeper love. But we cannot easily put it on a lunchbox, and we cannot sequel it, because it was never ours.  
   
And here is the tell, the single clearest sign that the new bonds are not forming fast enough. Watch what a culture does when it stops inventing: it starts eating its own past. The music critic Simon Reynolds named this "retromania" in his 2011 book, pop culture addicted to reruns, reissues, reboots, remakes, so hooked on its own memory that it can no longer imagine a genuinely new future. He called it a "nostalgia for a lost future," a culture becoming "a museum of itself." Now look at Disney's dominant strategy of the last decade: taking the animated films we loved as a child and selling them back to we as live-action, as sequels, as legacy. That is retromania as a business model. It is the flywheel running not on new fire but on stored fuel, on the love it planted decades ago in adults who are now old enough to buy the tickets. A wheel greased by nostalgia keeps turning. It just doesn't generate anything new to turn on. And we cannot imprint a child with a remake of the thing that imprinted their parents. Nostalgia only works on people who already have the memory.  
So the founding idea survives, but in a harder, more honest form than the committee's opening paragraph. Not "shared culture is dead." Rather: *the machine that turned shared culture into money assumed it would keep planting fresh love in each new generation of children, and that first, load-bearing assumption is the one now in question.*  
   
**How Disney actually makes money (the flywheel)**  
To govern this company we must understand the engine, because every decision in this room either feeds it or starves it.  
Disney's genius was never a single film. It was a flywheel, a self-reinforcing loop where each part feeds the next:

1. A character or story is created and imprints on a child.  
2. That love drives them to films (theatrical and streaming).  
3. Which drives them to the parks, to stand inside the story.  
4. Which drives them to merchandise and consumer products, to take the story home.  
5. Which deepens the love, and sells the next film, ride, and toy, spinning the wheel again.

The whole loop depends on stage one: children forming an emotional bond with characters Disney owns. Every other stage monetizes that bond. Parks, merchandise, sequels, and streaming are all ways of charging rent on a love that was planted in childhood.  
   
Now see where the relevance crisis attacks. If children are forming their deepest bonds with a YouTuber, a Roblox avatar, or a game Disney does not own, the flywheel loses its first stage. The films, parks, and merchandise can coast for a while on the affection of adults who imprinted decades ago, which is exactly what is happening, and why nostalgia is doing so much of the work. But a flywheel powered by aging fans is a flywheel winding down. The strategic question underneath everything is: can Disney still plant love in a child who lives inside an algorithm it does not control?  
   
**Who tells the bedtime story now?**  
Underneath the attention war and the streaming math and the theme-park prices, underneath the political fights that will eat half our committee, there is one question, and it is not really a business question at all:  
Can Disney still plant love in a child who lives inside an algorithm it does not own? And if the honest answer is no, what is Disney willing to *become* instead?  
   
Everything on the floor is a version of that question. Every fight is an argument about it in disguise. The delegates who win this committee will not be the ones who best defend the old machine. They will be the ones who understand, coldly and early, that they may be custodians of a beautiful thing whose founding conditions have quietly expired, and who decide, with open eyes, what to save and what to let go.  
   
For a hundred years, one company held a genuinely strange power: a large share of the human race let it choose the myths their children grew up inside. That was never natural, never permanent, and never as universal as it looked from the center. Now the job of giving children their first stories is being handed to creators, to games, to strangers, to code, and to countries that used to only *receive* the myths and now *make* them. Maybe that is a catastrophe. Maybe it is a liberation. Maybe, as with most true things, it is both at once, depending on whether we are standing at the center or on the edge.  
   
Our job in the room is not to decide which. Our job is to notice that the ground has moved, to refuse the comfortable lie that another hit movie will fix it, and to ask the harder question out loud before the person across the table asks it first. The campfire has moved, and someone else is tending it now. The whole committee is arguing about whether Disney can build a new fire or whether it should learn, at last, to live without being the one everyone gathers around.  
   
 

 

**Chapter 3**  
   
**The number that lies**  
Start with a fact that should confuse us, because confusion is the correct first response to Disney in 2026\.  
In calendar 2025 Disney *won.* It was the only studio on Earth to cross $6 billion at the global box office, roughly $6.5 billion, its best year since 2019, while its nearest rival, Warner Bros., limped in around $4.3 billion. Three films passed a billion dollars each. About seven hundred million human beings sat in a dark room in front of a Disney movie. On the earnings call, the machine hummed. Buybacks flowed. The numbers said: *we are fine.*  
Now look at *what* won. The three billion-dollar hits were Zootopia 2 (a sequel), Lilo & Stitch (a live-action remake of a 2002 cartoon), and Avatar: Fire and Ash (the third film in one man's decades-long franchise). Every single pillar of the triumph was stored fuel: old love, planted years or decades ago, dug up and sold back. The franchise engine that was supposed to be the future (Marvel) kept misfiring. Meanwhile the one genuinely new, original thing Disney's animation studios tried that year, Pixar's *Elio*, grossed about $154 million and ranks among the lowest openings in Pixar's history.  
   
This is the whole crisis in one year of grosses. Disney is winning the present with the past, and it has forgotten how to make the future. The scoreboard is bright and the fuel tank is draining, and those two facts are not in tension, they are the *same* fact, seen from two distances. Hold that as we walk through the five fires. Each one, underneath, is another version of it.  
   
**Front 1: Attention War**  
Disney is losing the only customer that matters (the children) and it is not a customer it can win back with a good movie. By recent counts, YouTube is the favorite platform of roughly eight in ten young kids, one industry study of two-to-twelve-year-olds put YouTube's reach far ahead of streaming apps, with 82% of six-to-nine-year-olds naming it their top choice. Disney+ reaches a real but much smaller share. And here is the part that should keep a Disney executive awake: the kids are not just watching *different screens.* They are forming a different kind of bond.  
   
A Disney character lives in a fictional world we look *into.* A YouTuber lives in *ours*, and looks *out*, into the camera, at us, saying "you," "us," "we guys," asking for a comment, feeling like a friend who happens to be famous. Sociologists named this one-directional intimacy "parasocial interaction" back in 1956 (Donald Horton and Richard Wohl), and Disney was, for a century, the greatest parasocial engine ever built: we loved Cinderella and she never changed, never aged, never let us down, never got cancelled. But the bond a ten-year-old forms today is increasingly with MrBeast, a single person with more than 400 million subscribers who is now, functionally, a television network, a snack company, and a charity at once. To that child he does not feel like a story. He feels like a neighbor who might, at any moment, give *them* a car. The affection is warmer, more personal, more *real* than anything a princess can offer. And Disney cannot manufacture it, because it was never Disney's to make.  
   
Then there is the deeper shift, the one the writers on the attention economy have been warning about for years. The economist Herbert Simon saw it in 1971: when information becomes infinite, attention becomes the scarce thing, \<cite index="0-0"\>a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention\</cite\>. Tim Wu, in *The Attention Merchants* (2016), traced the whole grim business of harvesting that scarce thing and selling it. But the newest twist is that children are no longer just *receiving* stories at all. In Roblox, Minecraft, and Fortnite, they build their own worlds. A generation that authors its own universe at seven finds a pre-made twenty-two-minute cartoon slow, a thing that happens *to* we rather than a thing we *do.* We cannot easily imprint an author. We can only give them tools.  
   
**The mechanics, and the dilemma with no clean answer.** Disney has two obvious bad options and has quietly tried a third. It can *chase the children onto the platforms*, pour Elsa and Spider-Man into YouTube, Roblox, and short-form feeds. But there Disney is just another vendor renting space on someone else's algorithm, competing with a million free creators, unable to charge the park-and-merchandise rents that the flywheel runs on, and, worst of all, teaching kids to consume Disney the way they consume disposable content, swiped past in a second. Or it can *defend the walled garden*, its own films, channels, and app, which protects the flywheel's economics but surrenders the actual attention of an entire generation to competitors. Retreat and we shrink; advance and we cheapen ourself into a commodity.  
   
The third path is the interesting one, and we should know the current CEO personally championed it. In February 2024 Disney put $1.5 billion into Epic Games, the maker of Fortnite, its biggest bet on gaming ever, to build a "persistent, open, interoperable" universe where kids could *create their own* stories using Disney characters. The logic: don't fight the world-building platforms, don't merely rent space on them, buy our way *into* the campfire itself, and let children build with our bricks. It is genuinely clever. It may also be a trap, because as one analyst warned, the eight-year-olds of today may not care about Fortnite in six years, and we cannot own relevance by renting the platform that currently holds it. This is the front's real question, and no delegate should pretend it has a settled answer: can we plant durable, ownable love in a child through a tool they use to make things we don't control?  
   
My own read, offered as an opinion and not a fact: the attention war is the one front where the old flywheel logic actively misleads. Every instinct Disney has, own the character, control the world, charge the rent, is an instinct built for an audience that *receives.* The child of 2026 participates. A company organized around telling children stories is trying to survive in a world where children would rather be told they matter.  
   
**Front 2: Quality Problem (the loss of "magic")**  
For a decade Disney's answer to creative risk was to stop taking it. Buy Pixar (2006), Marvel (2009), Lucasfilm (2012); turn beloved, pre-loved properties into an assembly line; and let the brand do the work that used to be done by a story nobody had seen before. For a while it was the most successful strategy in the history of entertainment. By 2026 the assembly line is throwing sparks.  
   
The evidence is a run of misses that would have been unthinkable in 2016\. Thunderbolts\*, critically praised, one of the better-reviewed Marvel films in years, limped to roughly $382 million worldwide against a production budget near $180 million, needing an estimated $425 million just to break even. The cruel detail: days after it opened, Bob Iger publicly called it the "first and best example" of Marvel's new strategy. Six weeks later it had cratered. The Marvels (2023) reportedly lost around $237 million, one of the biggest bombs in the studio's history. Pixar's original bet flatlined. Snow White, a flagship live-action fairy-tale remake, became a notorious failure that lost over $100 million amid years of controversy and reshoots. Forbes ran a line that should be carved over the studio door: over two years, Disney lost roughly $619 million in average takings *per Marvel film* compared with the pre-pandemic peak. Before COVID, nineteen of twenty-two Marvel films cracked $500 million. Since 2020, only about half did. Industry analysts now say out loud what would once have been heresy: audiences are suffering franchise fatigue, we can feed people the same universe only so many times before wonder curdles into homework.  
   
Here is where the critics matter. In 2019 Martin Scorsese wrote an essay for *The New York Times* arguing that franchise tentpoles were closer to theme parks than to cinema, engineered, risk-averse products, crowding out the strange and the singular. He was mocked as an old man yelling at capes. He was also, on the numbers, early. And the philosopher Mark Fisher, in *Capitalist Realism* (2009), gave the deeper name for it: a culture that can no longer imagine anything genuinely new, so it endlessly reheats what already exists. Disney did not invent this condition. It industrialized it.  
   
**The mechanics, and the nuance the headlines miss.** Two things complicate the doom. First, because of the flywheel (see the first essay), a Marvel "flop" is *not* a pure loss the way a normal studio's flop is. The film seeds a Disney+ subscription, a toy line, a park ride, a cruise theme, and a marketing runway for the next film, as Variety noted, Marvel is uniquely cushioned because no single movie's box office is the whole story. Second, look again at what *did* work in 2025: Avatar. Its sequels arrive *years* apart, and they hold their value precisely because they are scarce. The lesson analysts keep drawing is that Disney's problem may not be "known IP" at all, it may be volume. Flood the market with Marvel and each entry is worth less; ration Avatar and each is an event. Scarcity, it turns out, was part of what people were paying for. So the "quality problem" might be better named a quantity problem, the brand diluted by its own abundance, which loops straight back to W. David Marx's argument from the first essay that frictionless abundance strips scarcity and strips value with it.  
The dilemma with no clean answer. Keep mining known IP and we get reliable-ish returns while accelerating fatigue, cheapening the brand, and, this is the quiet killer, *making no new characters for the flywheel.* Every remake is a character the studio is *spending*, not *planting.* Bet on new original worlds and most will fail expensively (ask Elio), but original hits are the only way to refill a vault that Disney has been withdrawing from for twenty years without a deposit. The company is strip-mining a mountain it has forgotten how to grow. My opinion: this is the most *existential* of the five fronts and the least urgent-looking, which is exactly why a room full of people optimizing this quarter will neglect it until it is too late.  
   
**Front 3: Streaming Trap (the broken cash machine)**  
The old monoculture came with a perfect, almost embarrassingly profitable money machine bolted to its side: the cable bundle. Understand the bundle and we understand why its death is a knife specifically shaped for Disney.  
The bundle was a genius piece of hidden cross-subsidy. Tens of millions of households paid every month for a fat package of channels *whether or not they watched them.* The person who only wanted the Food Network still paid, invisibly, for ESPN. That meant ESPN collected enormous per-subscriber "carriage fees" from a huge base of people who never watched a single game. It was, quietly, one of the great money engines in media history, and it funded everything else Disney did.  
   
That engine is dying, and worse, what replaces it is a *worse business.* Media analyst Matthew Ball has written some of the clearest work on why: streaming customers subscribe for one show and cancel the next month (churn), the content costs a fortune, nobody pays for channels they don't watch, and the pricing power that the bundle hid is now brutally exposed. Disney is stranded halfway across this bridge, bleeding profit from the shrinking old model while its streaming business only recently clawed toward profitability (Disney+ turned a profit in 2024). And in late 2025 the deepest danger showed its face. When Disney's carriage deal with YouTube TV expired on 30 October 2025, Disney pulled its channels, ESPN, ABC, FX, National Geographic, the SEC Network, off the platform for about fifteen days, the longest blackout in Disney's history, cutting roughly ten million subscribers off from live sports in the middle of college football season and costing Disney a reported $4.3 million a day.  
   
But the money was not the real story. The real fight was over a word: ingestion, whether ESPN's new streaming service would live *inside* Google's store, where Google would own the viewer data and the customer relationship. Disney fought that with everything, because it saw the abyss clearly: if the tech platform owns the data and the customer, Disney becomes a mere *supplier*, exactly the "just another vendor on someone else's algorithm" fate from Front 1, now arriving for the grown-up business too. The blackout revealed the terrifying new fact of the industry: for fifty years Disney dictated terms to distributors. Now a Google-owned streamer that had *five* carriage fights in a single year can shrug and say, as one analyst put it, there's a bigger power player in town.  
   
**The mechanics, and the dilemma.** ESPN has launched its own expensive direct-to-consumer service (August 2025\) as a hedge against cord-cutting, and cut staff to absorb the losses. Loud voices, including activist investors, argue Disney should simply amputate: sell or spin off the shrinking television and sports arms, bank the cash, stop the bleeding. Others insist ESPN is irreplaceable, because live sports is the last monoculture Disney still owns, one of the very few things tens of millions of people still watch at the same moment, together, live, un-skippable, ad-intact. So: manage the transition slowly and bleed for years while the market loses patience, or break the empire apart, please Wall Street, raise cash, and in the process surrender the one asset that still gathers a shared audience. The devil's-advocate wrinkle to press in the room: even *sports* is now fragmenting into streaming exclusives and a generation of "cord-nevers." If live sports is the last campfire, notice that it too is being carried, log by log, onto platforms Disney doesn't own.  
   
**Front 4: The Parks (Disney’s golden goose)**  
The parks are the cash cow that pays for the entire war: real, physical, enormously profitable (a record \~$10 billion in quarterly Experiences revenue at the start of fiscal 2026). And Disney has been squeezing that goose to the edge of what a goose will tolerate.  
   
The numbers have a cruelty to them. A single day at the Magic Kingdom now runs around $200 a head before a bite of food; a top-tier paid after-hours event adds nearly another $200; a multi-generational week can top $15,000. Free perks that existed a few years ago, the old FastPass, were replaced by paid line-skipping (Genie+, Lightning Lane). And here is the statistic that should stop a delegate cold: a 2024 LendingTree survey found that roughly one in four Disney visitors took on debt to fund the trip, rising to 45% of parents of young children, up from 30% two years earlier, averaging nearly $2,000 in debt. *The New Yorker* profiled the people living this: a college freshman who burned through $15,000 in savings on six trips and ended with five dollars to her name; a couple $70,000 in the hole.  
   
This is where the anthropologists earn their keep, because a Disney park was never just a business, it was a cathedral. The sociologist Alan Bryman coined "Disneyization" (2004) to describe how theming, relentless merchandising, and performed cheerfulness spread from the parks to the whole economy. Jean Baudrillard, decades earlier, called Disneyland the purest example of the "hyperreal", a fake so complete it makes the ordinary world outside feel *more* real by comparison. Umberto Eco called it an "absolute fake" that gives us more than reality. And Henry Giroux, in *The Mouse That Roared* (1999), named the ache underneath the price tag with a phrase worth memorizing: the parks sell "the swindle of fulfillment." People go into debt for it the way people once tithed. One writer who studies Disney superfans compares the whole subculture, unironically, to a church, complete with "elder" status earned through devotion and spending. "My retirement plan," one fan posted, "is just vibes and pixie dust."  
   
**The mechanics, and the dilemma.** Every price hike *works*, until it doesn't. In the most recent quarter, US parks revenue rose about 6% while attendance *fell* about 1%. Read that carefully: Disney is extracting more money from fewer people. That is the signature of a luxury good, Thorstein Veblen's "conspicuous consumption," where a high price is part of the appeal, and for a while it is wonderful for margins. But it quietly breaks the founding promise that Disney is *for every family*, and it converts a mass sacrament into a rich person's ritual. Layer on a self-inflicted wound: a 2024 change to the parks' disability-access program (DAS) triggered a class-action ADA lawsuit filed in early 2025, a shareholder proposal, and years of coverage, the "magic is for everyone" brand caught arguing in federal court about *who counts as disabled enough* to skip a line. So: keep raising prices and IP-branding every new land to fund the streaming losses and legal bills, and erode the accessible, magical, universal brand that is the entire reason the parks command a premium. Or hold and cut prices to protect the brand, and starve the one reliable engine paying for all the other fires. The parks are simultaneously the strongest business in the company and the place where its soul is most visibly for sale.  
   
**Front 5: Political Siege**  
Disney has become a target in a national culture war, and this time the government holds a weapon pointed at a genuinely vital organ. The bare facts, verified and dry, because the dryness is the point. In March 2025 the Federal Communications Commission, under Trump-appointed chairman Brendan Carr, opened an investigation into Disney/ABC's diversity, equity, and inclusion practices, alleging "invidious" discrimination. In late April 2026 the FCC escalated dramatically: it ordered Disney to file early renewal applications for the broadcast licenses of its eight owned ABC television stations, licenses that weren't otherwise due until 2028 to 2031\. The FCC had not made such an early-renewal move in *decades.* Disney filed "under protest," calling the order "unlawful, arbitrary, and unconstitutional." A separate FCC inquiry targets the daytime show *The View* over "equal time" rules. And the timing lit up every alarm: the license move came right after President and First Lady Trump called for the firing of late-night host Jimmy Kimmel over his jokes, a suspension-and-reinstatement saga that had already triggered a consumer boycott, with canceled Disney+ subscriptions and canceled park trips. The lone Democratic FCC commissioner, Anna Gomez, called the license review "clearly a pretext" and "the most egregious" First Amendment violation the agency had ever committed.  
   
Now the nuance that separates a panicked delegate from a sharp one, and it cuts *against* the "existential threat" framing while making the situation somehow worse. Disney will almost certainly not lose its licenses. As a communications lawyer told CNN, the legal standard for denying a license renewal is "almost insurmountable," and a hearing plus judicial review would grind on for years, during which the stations keep broadcasting normally. So why do it? Because the process itself is the punishment. The political scientist Malcolm Feeley wrote a whole book on this idea in 1979, that in many systems the penalty is not the verdict but the ordeal of being dragged through the machinery. The legal name for what's happening here is jawboning, informal government pressure that coerces speech without ever formally banning it, and its purpose is the chilling effect: not to take the license, but to make Disney, and every other broadcaster watching, quietly softer, more careful, less willing to air the joke or the segment that draws fire. The Freedom of the Press Foundation put it plainly: the First Amendment does not permit broadcast licenses to be used as weapons to punish protected speech.  
   
**The dilemma with no clean answer.** Fight the government on free-speech principle, legally righteous, morally clean, and *guaranteed* to enrage the administration and roughly half the audience. Or quietly appease, settle, soften, sacrifice a host or a policy, which looks like surrender, betrays our own talent and employees, and enrages the *other* half. There is no move that doesn't cost a slice of the country. And here is the sociological knife underneath it, the one that ties this front back to the first essay: Disney's century-old superpower was universality, it belonged to *everyone.* In a nation that no longer agrees on anything, universality is no longer an asset. It is a liability. The company built to be loved by all is now perfectly positioned to be resented by all. What made Disney Disney is now what makes it a target.  
   
**The Vice**  
Step back from the five fires and look at their shared shape, because the shape is the whole committee.  
Every front pits short-term financial health against long-term cultural survival, and pulls them in opposite directions. Cut costs, buy back stock, mine known IP, raise park prices, amputate money-losing divisions: every one of these makes *this quarter's* numbers better, and every one of them spends down the exact things Disney needs to survive the *century*, new characters, creative risk, an accessible beloved brand, the affection of the next child. Relevance requires expensive, patient, failure-prone bets that hurt the very numbers the market is grading us on right now. So the numbers say "we are fine" while the culture says "we are dying," and the people in the room who trust the numbers and the people who trust the culture will never agree on a single vote. That disagreement is not a bug in the committee. It is the committee.  
   
This is not a Disney quirk. It is one of the best-documented pathologies in modern capitalism, and knowing the names turns a vague unease into a precise argument we can win a debate with.  
Start with John Maynard Keynes, who in 1936 compared the stock market to a newspaper beauty contest where we win not by picking the prettiest face but by guessing which face *everyone else will guess*, investors price not what a company is worth, but what they think other investors will think next quarter. That is *why* the market rewards the short term: it is a machine for guessing other people's guesses. Then Clayton Christensen, whose *Innovator's Dilemma* (1997) showed that great incumbent companies fail not by being stupid but by being *rational*, they listen to their best current customers, optimize their existing profitable business, and starve the weird small disruptive thing until it grows up and eats them. In "The Capitalist's Dilemma" (HBR, 2014\) he sharpened it: firms increasingly chase efficiency gains that free up cash, then hand that cash back to shareholders instead of pouring it into the risky, market-*creating* innovations that make new customers. Which is precisely William Lazonick's charge in "Profits Without Prosperity" (HBR, 2014): the buyback economy, "downsize and distribute", quietly converts a company's future into a present-day payout for shareholders. Dominic Barton called the whole disease "quarterly capitalism" (HBR, 2011). And underneath all of them sits Joseph Schumpeter's 1942 gale of "creative destruction," the storm that eventually blows down every incumbent that has grown too comfortable to reinvent itself.  
   
We do not have to reach for theory to see this operating at Disney. In 2024 the activist investor Nelson Peltz waged a proxy fight demanding sharper margins, cost discipline, and a clean succession, the market, personified, knocking on the boardroom door and asking for the century to be spent faster. He lost the vote. But the pressure he represented never left the room; it *is* the room. And remember the number that opened this essay: the 2025 box-office crown, won on a sequel, a remake, and one auteur's franchise. That is what winning the quarter looks like. It looks like a triumph. It is the sound of the vault being emptied to make the scoreboard glow.  
   
So the real question the Vice forces on every delegate is not "how do we fix Disney." It is colder than that: when the numbers and the culture disagree, which one do we serve, and who in this room gets rich from our answer? Because different seats profit from different answers. The person who wants the spin-off, the person who wants the buyback, the person who wants ten more years of remakes, each is optimizing something real, and each is quietly choosing which of Disney's two clocks to obey. The committee is sixteen hours of that choice, made over and over, by people who will not admit they are making it.  
   
**Fractured House**  
Now add the accelerant, the company facing all of this just changed leaders. On 18 March 2026, Josh D'Amaro, the head of the theme-parks division, a twenty-eight-year Disney veteran of the *retail* side of the house, became CEO. Sit with the irony: a company staring down a creativity crisis, a relevance crisis, a *"can we still make new stories children love"* crisis, just handed the top job to an operations-and-parks man. And it did so once before. The *last* parks chief promoted to CEO was Bob Chapek, whose tenure detonated so badly that the board dragged Bob Iger out of retirement to clean it up. The board knows this. Chairman James Gorman, the former Morgan Stanley chief whose entire reputation now rests on this handoff *not* becoming Chapek 2.0, went on television to promise, "We won't have the same drama we had last time." That he had to say it tells us what everyone fears.  
Meanwhile the runner-up did not lose so much as *reposition.* Dana Walden, the television-and-streaming boss widely seen as the other finalist, became President and Chief Creative Officer, taking oversight of the film business, which means the person who lost the CEO race now runs the exact creative engine whose failure is Front 2, and reports to a parks executive who has never greenlit a movie. And Bob Iger, 74, the retiring legend, stays on the board and as senior advisor through the end of 2026, still in the building, still on the phone, still the man whose shadow every decision is measured against. This is not a united command facing a hard problem. It is a probationary CEO, a consolation-prize deputy holding the creative keys, a founder-figure who won't quite leave, and a chairman betting his legacy on the whole arrangement holding together.  
   
The devil's-advocate case, which a good delegate should be able to argue even while distrusting it, is that D'Amaro is *exactly right* for this moment. The flywheel begins with a child's bond, and no one alive understands how children and families physically experience the Disney brand better than the man who ran the parks for years. Maybe the crisis of the first stage is best solved by someone who has spent his career at that first stage. Or maybe that's the comforting story a board tells itself right before it repeats the mistake it swore it had learned from. Nobody in the room knows which. That not-knowing is the fuel.  
   
**Epilog: five fires, one machine**  
Return, finally, to where the first chapter left us. The relevance crisis (the dissolving of shared culture, the question of whether Disney can still plant love in a child inside an algorithm it doesn't own) is the *ground.* The five fronts are the *fires* burning on that ground: attention, quality, streaming, parks, politics. And the Vice is the *machine* that guarantees every attempt to put out one fire feeds another, because the market grades the quarter and the culture grades the century, and the two scoreboards disagree about every move we could make.  
   
We will be tempted, in the heat of the room, to treat these as five separate problems with five separate fixes. Resist that. They are five pressure points on a single body, and the body's deepest condition is the one no earnings call will name: a machine built to manufacture shared childhood, running in a world that no longer shares, no longer sits still to be told a story, and no longer agrees on anything long enough to love the same mouse.  
   
Our job is not to save the old machine. It may not be savable in its old form, and the delegates who grasp that first will run the room. Our job is to decide, with open eyes and a cold heart, what Disney is willing to stop being in order to still exist, a film studio, a television company, a price every family can afford, the thing everyone gathers around, and to make that choice out loud, before the person across the table makes it for us. The scoreboard is bright. The tank is draining. Now go argue about which one is real.  
   
   
   
   
**The state of play, 5 September 2026**  
As we take our seats: the box-office year is being won on sequels and spectacle while the next generation drifts to creators and games. Streaming inches toward profit as the old cable engine keeps dying. The government's license review hangs over the television business like a blade. The parks are printing cash and burning goodwill. The activist voices calling for a breakup are getting louder, and the market is watching the new CEO for any sign of weakness.  
   
Nothing here has to be solved in order. Everything here feeds everything else. The clock runs one month per hour toward a final reckoning, and the fires spread while we argue.  
**Sources and rabbit holes**  
*Read these sources against each other. The partisans will try to tell us Disney is either sacred or sinister. The truth, as always, lives in the tension between the two — and the tension is where our arguments will be strongest in the committee room.*  
   
**On Snow White and the founding wager**

* Neal Gabler, *Walt Disney: The Triumph of the American Imagination* (Knopf, 2006). The definitive biography, unflinching on Walt's autocratic management style and his willingness to endanger his employees' livelihoods for his creative vision. The Bank of America loan scene is reconstructed in detail.  
* Richard Schickel, *The Disney Version: The Life, Times, Art and Commerce of Walt Disney* (1968, revised 1985). The first serious critical assessment of Disney as cultural phenomenon. Schickel was suspicious of the mythology decades before suspicion became fashionable.  
* The production details — exact budget ($1,488,422.74), the number of artists (500+), the number of paint shades (1,500), the Ward Kimball anecdote, the "Disney's Folly" epithet — are well-documented across multiple sources and confirmed on the film's Wikipedia page, which is unusually well-sourced for an animated film.  
* On Snow White's influence by German Expressionism: Walt's staff were explicitly told to study *Nosferatu* and *Caligari*. This is documented in the Disney Archives and confirmed in multiple production histories. Think about what this means: the founding act of "wholesome" American animation was directly inspired by the most disturbing films in European cinema.

**On the ABC-Disneyland deal**

* Richard Snow, *Disney's Land: Walt Disney and the Invention of the Amusement Park That Changed the World* (Scribner, 2019). The best account of the deal's financial architecture. David Senra's podcast *Founders* has an episode on this book worth listening to.  
* Mark Mason's Substack post "The Show That Built Disneyland" (May 2024\) on Stay Tuned. Compact and well-sourced on the ABC deal economics.  
* The American Business History Center's account includes the detail about ABC-Paramount's Leonard Goldenson reaching out to Texas theater baron Karl Hoblitzelle to guarantee the bank loan — a reminder that even landmark deals depend on personal networks.  
* D23, Disney's official fan organization, confirms the buyback: $7.5 million in 1960 for ABC's $500,000 investment. Roughly 15x return in six years.  
* Todd Zenger, "What Is the Theory of Our Firm?", *Harvard Business Review* (June 2013). The "corporate theory" framework applied to the 1957 Synergy Map. Essential reading for understanding why Disney is not just a collection of businesses but a system with a logic.  
* The Reforge Blog analysis "Disney's 60-Year-Old Growth Map Answers the Netflix Question" is worth reading alongside Zenger. It argues that Eisner's non-synergistic acquisitions (Miramax, ESPN under its original structure) weakened the flywheel, while Iger's acquisitions (Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm) strengthened it. The Fox acquisition fits uncomfortably between these two categories.

**On the Renaissance and the limits of monetization**

* Don Hahn (director), *Waking Sleeping Beauty* (Stone Circle Pictures / Walt Disney Studios, 2009). Documentary on the animation department from 1984 to 1994\. Essential viewing. Made by an insider who had the institutional access and the emotional honesty to show both the creative exhilaration and the personal devastation of the period.  
* Christopher Holifield, "Rethinking the Disney Renaissance," *Fantasy/Animation* (November 2023). Academic essay that challenges the standard periodization and argues the "Renaissance" was partly a marketing construct that Disney and its fan communities have promoted uncritically. Correctly notes that Eisner and Katzenberg initially considered shutting down animation.  
* TIME's 2014 retrospective "The Little Mermaid at 25" includes the detail about Katzenberg wanting to cut "Part of Our World" and the directors reminding him about "Over the Rainbow." One of those moments where a single creative decision — a song staying in or being cut — determines the fate of an institution.  
* The Katzenberg memo (1991) has been widely discussed. James B. Stewart's *DisneyWar* (Simon & Schuster, 2005\) is the most thorough account of the internal politics of the Eisner era, including the Katzenberg departure, the Ovitz disaster, and the governance failures.

**On Save Disney and corporate governance**

* James B. Stewart, *DisneyWar* (Simon & Schuster, 2005). The definitive account. Stewart had extraordinary access and the journalistic instinct to treat the Disney succession crisis as a Shakespearean drama about power, ego, and institutional decay.  
* The ABC News report on the March 2004 shareholder meeting confirms the 43% withheld vote and includes Eisner's quote: "I love this company." The gap between the sincerity of that statement and the damage Eisner had done to the institution is the whole lesson of the chapter.  
* Comcast's $54 billion hostile bid is documented in SEC filings and contemporaneous reporting in the *Wall Street Journal*, *New York Times*, and *The Economist*. The Economist's observation about "viciously perfect timing" captures the predatory logic precisely.  
* We Minored in Film's retrospective "Bad Blood: Looking Back at Comcast's Attempt to Buy Disney in 2004" (June 2018\) is a useful summary with the Shortform analysis providing additional context on Eisner's trajectory from savior to obstacle.

**On the Disney+ era, COVID, succession, and the present crisis**

* Matthew Ball, *The Netflix Effect* (and his ongoing essays on Ballmetaverse.com / MatthewBall.co). The most rigorous analysis of streaming economics, including the structural problems with the subscriber-growth model and the difference between subscriber count and enterprise value.  
* Brooks Barnes's reporting in the *New York Times* on the Chapek era, Iger's return, and the Florida political confrontation is essential. Barnes has covered Disney for over a decade and understands the institutional culture.  
* Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997) provides the theoretical framework for understanding why Disney+ may be cannibalizing the theatrical and parks businesses that historically generated the deepest emotional connections. The streaming service is the cheaper, more convenient product that disrupts the premium experience.  
* On the Reedy Creek dissolution and Disney's political entanglement: the *Wall Street Journal* and *Orlando Sentinel* covered the legal and legislative maneuvering in detail. The key insight for delegates is that Disney's fifty-five years of quasi-governmental autonomy in Florida was not a natural right but a political concession that could be withdrawn — and was.

**Broader cultural and critical frameworks**

* Alan Bryman, *The Disneyization of Society* (SAGE, 2004). The sociological analysis of how Disney's organizational logic — theming, dedifferentiation of consumption, merchandising, emotional labor — has spread to non-Disney institutions. Useful for understanding why Disney crises feel like cultural crises, not just corporate ones.  
* Henry Giroux, *The Mouse That Roared: Disney and the End of Innocence* (Rowman & Littlefield, 1999, updated 2010). The ideological critique. Giroux argues that Disney's installation of values into children's minds is a political act that deserves scrutiny, not celebration. Read this against the Disney fan literature for productive friction.  
* Ariel Dorfman and Armand Mattelart, *How to Read Donald Duck: Imperialist Ideology in the Disney Comic* (1971, English translation 1975). The classic postcolonial analysis, written in Chile before the Pinochet coup. Argues that Disney comics in Latin America function as ideological instruments of American capitalism. Extreme in its claims, indispensable in its questions. Indonesian delegates may find the geopolitical dimension particularly relevant.  
* Simon Reynolds, *Retromania: Pop Culture's Addiction to Its Own Past* (Faber & Faber, 2011). Not specifically about Disney, but the best diagnosis of the cultural condition that makes the live-action remakes and franchise extensions feel simultaneously inevitable and exhausting.

**On the monoculture and its supposed death**

* Kyle Chayka, *Filterworld: How Algorithms Flattened Culture* (Doubleday, 2024). The core "fragmentation *and* homogenization" argument. Start with his 2016 essay "Welcome to AirSpace" (The Verge) if we want the short version.  
* W. David Marx, *Status and Culture* (2022) and *Blank Space: A Cultural History of the Twenty-First Century* (2025). The static-monoculture, reboot-and-sequel thesis. He also writes the Substack "Culture: An Owner's Manual."  
* *Foreign Policy*, "W. David Marx's *Blank Space* Review: Are We Really in a Global Monoculture?" (Jan 2026). The rebuttal, that we've never been further from a monoculture, and that the doom is a status panic. Read this *against* Marx.  
* Chris Anderson, *The Long Tail* (2006). Why infinite shelf space turns hits into niches. The economic backbone of the "return to normal" argument.  
* The YouTube video-essay genre. Search "the death of the monoculture" and we'll find dozens made between 2024 and 2026, including skeptical ones like "is the Monoculture REALLY dead?" Note the irony worth a paragraph of its own: worrying about lost shared culture has itself become a shared cultural obsession.

**On Disney specifically**

* Walt Disney's 1957 "Synergy Map." Look at the actual diagram (FlowingData and many business blogs reproduce it). See Todd Zenger's Harvard Business Review work on "corporate theory" for why it's taught.  
* Ariel Dorfman & Armand Mattelart, *How to Read Donald Duck* (1971; reissued by Pluto Press, 2019). The Global South reading of Disney as ideology. Essential, provocative, and contested, read it critically, not devotionally.  
* Henry Giroux, *The Mouse That Roared: Disney and the End of Innocence*. Disney as a teacher of values, not a neutral entertainer.

**On childhood, media, and the bond**

* Neil Postman, *The Disappearance of Childhood* (1982). Childhood as a thing media invents and media can dissolve.  
* Donald Horton & Richard Wohl, "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction" (1956). The origin of "parasocial", the one-way intimacy we feel toward a screen.

**On nostalgia and cultural repetition**

* Simon Reynolds, *Retromania: Pop Culture's Addiction to Its Own Past* (2011). "Nostalgia for a lost future"; culture as "a museum of itself." Maps almost perfectly onto Disney's remake strategy. Mark Fisher's writing on "hauntology" is the darker companion read.

**On where the children actually are (data, handle with care)**

* Pew Research Center, Common Sense Media (2025 Census), eMarketer, the Annie E. Casey Foundation, and Precise TV's "PARK" kids-media report. The recurring findings: YouTube dominates young viewers and is overtaking traditional TV for kids; Roblox and gaming are enormous; Disney+ reaches a real but much smaller share. **But**, the same data shows under-twelves still watch the most linear TV of any young cohort, often with family. Cite the nuance, not just the headline. And distrust any too-clean single number (see the "70%" warning above); always ask who ran the survey and who paid for it.

**On attention, creators, and the child (Front 1\)**

* Donald Horton & Richard Wohl, "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction" (1956), the origin of the "parasocial" bond a child forms with a screen figure.  
* Herbert Simon (1971), the source of "attention economy" thinking: infinite information makes attention the scarce resource.  
* Tim Wu, *The Attention Merchants* (2016), the business of harvesting and selling attention, from newspapers to feeds.  
* Gen Alpha data: Precise TV's "PARK" report (2025), eMarketer, Common Sense Media, Pew, the Annie E. Casey Foundation. Handle the exact percentages with care and always name the source, and remember the counter-fact from the first essay: young kids still watch a lot of traditional TV, often with family.  
* Disney's $1.5B Epic Games / Fortnite investment (Feb 2024): read Disney's own release *against* Forbes' skeptical take ("the eight-year-olds of today may not care about Fortnite in six years"). The disagreement is the strategy question.

**On quality, franchise fatigue, and the flop that isn't a flop (Front 2\)**

* Martin Scorsese, "I Said Marvel Movies Aren't Cinema. Let Me Explain." (*The New York Times*, 2019), the case that franchise cinema is closer to a theme park than an art form. Read the backlash too.  
* Mark Fisher, *Capitalist Realism* (2009), why a culture stops being able to imagine the genuinely new. The theoretical floor under "franchise fatigue."  
* Box office specifics: Variety, Forbes, Deadline, *The Hollywood Reporter*, Screen International. Verified figures used above, Thunderbolts\* \~$382M vs. \~$180M budget; Disney's \~$6.5B / No. 1 studio in 2025 on Zootopia 2, Lilo & Stitch, and Avatar; Pixar's *Elio* \~$154M. Note Variety's key nuance: the flywheel cushions any single film's loss.

**On the bundle, streaming, and the power flip (Front 3\)**

* Matthew Ball (matthewball.co), the sharpest independent writing on why streaming is a structurally worse business than the cable bundle it replaced. Start here for the economics.  
* The YouTube TV–Disney blackout (Oct–Nov 2025): Deadline, Slate, eMarketer, Variety. Watch specifically for the word "ingestion", the real fight was over who owns the viewer data, not the fee.  
* The framing of live sports as "the last monoculture" is worth interrogating, not just repeating: even sports is fragmenting onto platforms Disney doesn't control.

**On the parks as cathedral and commodity (Front 4\)**

* Henry Giroux, *The Mouse That Roared: Disney and the End of Innocence* (1999), "the swindle of fulfillment." The moral X-ray of the parks.  
* Alan Bryman, *The Disneyization of Society* (2004), theming, merchandising, and performed labor as an economic model.  
* Jean Baudrillard, *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981) and Umberto Eco, *Travels in Hyperreality* (1986), Disneyland as the "hyperreal" and the "absolute fake." The anthropology of a manufactured place that feels realer than real.  
* Thorstein Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899), "conspicuous consumption," for why a high price can be a feature.  
* The debt data: the LendingTree 2024 survey (\~1 in 4 visitors; 45% of parents of young kids), reported and humanized by *The New Yorker*. The DAS lawsuit: AP, U.S. News (Dec 2025).

**On the political siege and the law (Front 5\)**

* Primary reporting on the FCC action: CNN, CBS News, *The Hollywood Reporter*, PBS NewsHour, CNBC (2025–2026). Note commissioner Anna Gomez's dissent and the Freedom of the Press Foundation's statement.  
* Malcolm Feeley, *The Process Is the Punishment* (1979), the academic frame that explains *why* an unwinnable license case is still a weapon.  
* On "jawboning" and the chilling effect: the legal concept of informal government coercion of speech (see the Supreme Court's 2024 *NRA v. Vullo* for the current doctrine). The point for the room: the danger is self-censorship, not license loss.

**On the Vice, short-termism, the deep engine (the central paradox)**

* John Maynard Keynes, *The General Theory* (1936), ch. 12, the market as a "beauty contest," which is *why* the quarter beats the century.  
* Clayton Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997) and Christensen & Derek van Bever, "The Capitalist's Dilemma" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2014), how *rational* incumbents starve their own future. The single most useful frame for this committee.  
* William Lazonick, "Profits Without Prosperity" (*HBR*, 2014), the buyback critique; "downsize and distribute."  
* Dominic Barton, "Capitalism for the Long Term" (*HBR*, 2011), the naming of "quarterly capitalism."  
* Joseph Schumpeter, *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942), "creative destruction," the storm that eventually finds every empire.  
* The 2024 Nelson Peltz / Trian proxy fight (widely covered; Trian lost the vote), short-termism made flesh, knocking on Disney's boardroom door.
